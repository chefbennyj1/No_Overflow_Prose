Fifty stories beneath the burning streets, the subterranean Mnemosyne Core was beginning to become unstable. For four weeks, Silas Blackwell had been sealed behind its locked blast doors and three feet of lead-lined concrete.  

He stood at his terminal, placing a bent cigarette between his lips and sparking his lighter. He inhaled deeply, watching the holographic screen on mute. The face of a public relations synthetic delivered the evening news, the background filled with the muzzle flashes of Corporate Renewal agents holding the line. 

He let the smoke go from his lungs as a red diagnostic warning flashed across his terminal. The engine's core singularity—the black hole he had manufactured to process the being in front of him—was expanding. 

On the corner of his screen, the digital clock display violently stuttered. It ticked from 23:41, jumped forward to 23:44, and snapped backward to 23:40. Time was fracturing.

"The core is collapsing," Blackwell said. He didn't look up from the screen. "Without the Vigil to maintain the core, the singularity is in a runaway state." 

"Indeed," Parity's voice resonated across the containment field. 

Blackwell looked up. It was the first time she had moved in weeks. She pushed her porcelain finger against the containment field, tracing the perimeter. 

"The void can will be contained, Doctor," she said.

Blackwell watched her fingers spark white and bronze against the barrier. "That electromagnetic shielding is unbreakable, Parity." He didn't like how comfortable she was inside the cage. "There is no physical way for escape."

Parity stopped tracing the field. She tilted her glassy head, her unblinking eyes locking onto him through the electric wall. "You know, Doctor, I have spent quite a bit of time in your cage." She said, her voice sounding across the electric field.
Blackwell's eyes shifted from her to the diagnostic readout. With every micro-stutter of the fracturing clock, the fields power draw fluctuated.  It was insignificant, but it fluctuated. The realization of his situation hit him. She was timing the power surges. 

"Long enough to get to know it very, very, well." Parity's voice was no longer tied to the electricity of the containment field. It rattled in the steel floor grates, in the glass of the pit, and in the steel of the server racks, and the bones in his ear. 

Blackwell  looked over at the cage. It was empty. 

He froze. The cigarette fell from his lips, spilling ash and burning tobacco down his shirt. He didn't brush it off. 

Parity stood inches from him.
