Silas Blackwell took a drag from his cigarette, the ember flaring against the dawn. He was an erratic figure; a mop of white hair blew untamed in the morning wind, contrasting with the sharp lines of his dark collared shirt and tie. Over it all, he wore a lab coat that whipped around his legs in the cold air. He kept his expression purposefully unreadable behind a pair of circular dark glasses. Despite the calculated perfection required of his station, the cigarette held between his tobacco-stained fingers betrayed an obsessive energy. From the wind-swept terrace of the Mnemosyne Lab, he watched his world.

He didn't own any part of this view. He didn't have the inheritance to buy a single square foot of the platinum composite he was standing on. The tower belonged to the "Company," Takeda-Holden Corporation—a monument of corporate wealth. But as the Head of Synthetic Integration, Blackwell knew this building belonged to him in ways the Company would never comprehend.

The night’s heavy rain had washed the air clean, leaving a settled mist beneath the high-altitude pedestrian tiers. He exhaled, the grey smoke blurring the Zen-like patterns etched into the company's alloy railing. There were no sprawling billboards up here; the corporate elite didn't need to be sold. The only illumination came from floating holographic displays, streaming diagnostics in silence. The polished walkways were empty, this early. The peak of New Veridia wasn't a metropolis; it was a sleeping monster, ready to awake.

There was no need to turn around to know he was no longer alone. The faint hiss of the glass doors sliding open behind him confirmed it.

"Are we ready, Miss Marlowe?" Blackwell asked, his voice calm, carrying over the wind.

Elena Marlowe stood in the threshold, framed by the cool glow of the massive holographic displays inside. She was an imposing presence, her lab coat a stark contrast against the dark server racks of the control room. Her natural hair framed a face set in unyielding focus. 

"Yes, Doctor Blackwell," she replied. Her golden eyes caught the reflection of the cascading data streams—striking, but Blackwell viewed them as precision corporate engineering. "The Mnemosyne Engine is connected to the city's main fusion reactor. You have three hundred and twenty terawatts at your disposal."

Blackwell raised the cigarette to his lips one last time, inhaling until the end burned close to the filter. He held the smoke in his lungs for a long second, then exhaled and dropped the butt over the edge of the railing.

The spent cigarette vanished into the abyss. To Blackwell, the fog was a necessary filter. It formed an opaque floor of vapor, swallowing the corroded foundations of the Dead Circuits—the crushed remains of Oshima. Decades ago, Takeda-Holden hadn't bothered to demolish the slums; they simply poured the concrete for New Veridia directly on top of them. He knew what lived down there in the dark, buried beneath his feet: a tangle of failing systems, and the millions scurrying through the forgotten city. Noise beneath the sleeping monster.

Blackwell turned and walked past Elena and into the lab. The air inside vibrated with a hum—the terrifying reality of the city's reactor channeling into the floor beneath their feet.

Past the primary control consoles, the floor was manned by a mix of human technicians and the lab's impassive synthetics. The constructs worked with rigid efficiency, anchoring the chaotic energy of the room. Blackwell approached the reinforced railing that encircled the center of the pit. Gripping the cold metal, he looked down.

Below him sat the Mnemosyne Engine—or at least, the very top of it. The staggering bulk of the machine was housed deep within the tower's core. What ascended through the building's spine, and protruded into this observation chamber was a cluster of stark white pillars of varying heights. They thrust sharply from the floor, terminating in angled points. A vertical display panel ran up one side of each pillar. Currently, every single screen was empty and black.

The floor of the engine pit was constructed of white paneling, though it was obscured. A heavy layer of fog rolled across the base of the pillars, breaking against the stark geometry like silent rapids before cascading over the edges like a waterfall. The air at the center of the array was bitter cold, a necessary extreme to keep the dormant core stabilized. The great engine was powered down, silent and waiting.

"A drop in the bucket for what we're asking it to unearth, Miss Marlowe," Blackwell said, his voice dropping into a gravelly rasp. He pulled a fresh cigarette from his coat pocket and slid it between his lips, though he didn't light it. 

Bracing his weight against the railing, he leaned forward, his reflection distorted in the reinforced glass. "The board wants better data compression. A smarter artificial intelligence to predict their margins. They think I'm building them a calculator."

A humorless breath fogged the glass. "I'm building a monument. Harnessing my singularity."

"Doctor Blackwell. Silas." Elena's voice interrupted, a note of warning creeping into her tone. "The board dialed in."

Above the primary consoles, a massive display flickered to life. The encrypted feed partitioned the screen into a grid of digital monitors. Each square displayed nothing but the featureless silhouette of a corporate executive.

"Perfect," Blackwell murmured, his lip curling into a sinister smile, "Just what we need. Extra eyes."

"The Takeda-Holden Corporation expects success today, Blackwell." a synthesized voice echoed from the grid.

Blackwell didn't bother to look back at the screen or at Elena, keeping his gaze fixed on the dormant core below. "Very well, Miss Marlowe," he murmured. "Give them a show."

Elena didn't blink. She remained where she was, her posture shifting from subordinate to commander as she leaned into the microphone of the control room.  There was a quick succession of two tones over the speaker system. Several technicians in white coats looked up from their consoles.

"All stations, synchronize for initialization." Elena's voice reverberated in a  crackle over the laboratory loudspeaker, almost robotic. "Initiate sequence."

The room erupted into a synchronized motion.

Below Blackwell, the engine responded. Dense fog pooling at the base of the pillars surged upward, flooding the pit as the massive cooling valves released a torrent of thick gas. Then, one by one, the black screens on the sides of the white pillars displayed the boot sequence. They flickered at first, before stabilizing into cascades of crisp white and blue text, flowing down each pillar, illuminating the swirling fog in a spectral light.

The reactor's hum shifted into a rhythmic pulse—a heavy vibration creeping through the concrete and into their bones. The very air in the lab felt thick with the sheer gravity of it.

Blackwell’s smile widened. He watched the data stream, entranced by the magnitude of the activation. He was calm, unaffected by the bone-rattling force of his creation. 

The Board watched from their remote sanctuaries, confident that distance would protect them. They didn't understand the physics. If the containment failed, the resulting rupture would swallow them as easily as the lab. 

Behind him, technicians on the floor exchanged nervous glances, shrinking back from the railing. They seemed unnerved by the sheer physical presence of the machine, but Blackwell remained unbothered. 

Elena stepped up to the railing, studying a datapad in her hand, her tense posture relaxing just a fraction. "The initialization sequence is holding steady," she said, a rare note of satisfaction bleeding into her voice. She stepped closer to him, so only he could hear, "The containment calculations were flawless, Silas. You calculated activation. Congratulations."

"Of course they were," Blackwell murmured, the unlit cigarette bobbing between his lips.

On the immense display above the floor, the grid of silhouettes leaned forward. A metallic murmur of approval—or at least, predatory interest—emanated from the feed. The Takeda-Holden Corporation was watching their investment come to life. Beside him, Elena stiffened under the executive gaze. To her, this was just a performance.

"Doctor Blackwell. Ma'am." 

The hushed voice interrupted the moment. Iris, a junior technician, scurried up behind them, not looking up from the glowing datapad in her hands. She was a stark contrast to the sterile perfection of the room. Her hair was unkempt, defying the lab's strict dress code, and heavy gold hoops swung from her ears. She shoved her black-rimmed glasses up the bridge of her nose—a compulsive tic she exhibited whenever forced away from her screens. 

"Ma'am, local space-time..," Iris stammered in a frantic whisper, shooting a fearful glance at the Board's display before hiding behind her tablet. "It's ..." She paused, her focus visibly fragmenting for a second, then continued. "...a mass-displacement anomaly. A severe fluctuation in the event horizon."

Elena spun around. She cast a quick glance up at the Board's display—a fleeting dread that the subordinate could ruin the demonstration—before her piercing eyes snapped back to Iris.

The moment hung in the air. Blackwell observed the exchange in silence, recognizing the severity of the girl's intrusion without anxiety, curious to see how Elena would discipline the disruption.

Elena stepped into Iris's personal space. She didn't even look at the data.

"Negative," Elena said, her voice dropping into a scolding hiss through gritted teeth. "That is a predicted resonance."

Iris flinched, pulling the tablet tight against her chest. She swallowed hard, her eyes darting between the towering machine and her supervisor. "Yes, ma'am. Of course. All systems nominal."

"I need the telemetry!" Elena demanded, pivoting away.

At the adjacent console, a synthetic stood at attention. The designation *L-One-Two* (Laboratory - 12) printed cleanly beneath its left eye. Its photoreceptive irises spun, emitting an eerie luminescence as they processed the influx. The construct shifted its gaze toward Blackwell, the motion disturbing the white fiber-optics rooted in its scalp. As the lenses locked onto his face, their rotation slowed into a semblance of admiration.

"Horizon stability confirmed," the machine reported.

Blackwell admired the synthetic. It existed solely for the objective, unburdened by human distractions. That ruthless efficiency was why he allowed Elena to share his pedestal. She had engineered the construct's cognitive and behavioral matrix, forging a mind as uncompromising as his own. Yet, he knew her design was nothing compared to the singularity he had just awakened.

"Fusion Control says the thermal limits are safe, Doctor," the synthetic stated, its voice flat. 

"Fusion Control doesn't understand the optics of my quantum singularity," Blackwell retorted.

A monotone voice echoed from the display grid above. "Satisfactory, Blackwell," a shadowed silhouette stated. "The Company commends your success."

Blackwell scoffed. "Humph."

Elena shot an apprehensive glance toward the panels. "What the Doctor means is, we are glad that today has been a success."

"Progress will be monitored," another executive replied. The connection severed.

"Mass Displacement Anomaly? That wasn't in the preliminary notes, Silas." Elena's true expression bled through as she looked at Blackwell, peering past his dark glasses.

Blackwell shifted his focus to her for the first time. "It's predicted resonance," he said, the corner of his mouth rising slightly.

Elena shook her head, dropping the dangerous subject. "You know, it wouldn't hurt you to talk to the Board. You are the lead."

Blackwell turned back to the pillar readouts. Amidst the scrolling data, a shadow process emerged — a branch of inverted engine code, and it was expanding. He caught the gaze of a nearby synthetic and gave a subtle nod. Its eyes flared, snapping its focus across the room to a second synthetic. Together they scrubbed the shadow process from the logs, silent, practiced, done before Elena could turn around.

"I don't like the Board," Blackwell said, keeping his gaze fixed on the engine.

"Funding, Silas," she urged. "Remember the funding."

"I don't like the Board," he repeated.

Elena exhaled, rubbing her temples. "And everybody knows it."
