Silas Blackwell took a drag from his cigarette, the ember flaring against the blues and ivory of the early morning sky. A mop of white hair blew untamed in the morning wind, contrasting the sharp, reverent lines of his dark vest and tie. Over it all, he wore a lab coat that whipped around his legs in the cold air. He stood behind a pair of circular dark glasses, breathing a thin stream of smoke back into the wind. From the wind-swept terrace of the Mnemosyne Lab, he watched his world.

He didn't own any part of this view. He didn't have the inheritance to buy a single square foot of the platinum composite he was standing on. The tower belonged to the "Company," Takeda-Holden Corporation—a monument of corporate wealth. But as the Head of Synthetic Integration, Blackwell knew this building belonged to him in ways the Company would never comprehend.

The night’s heavy rain had washed the air clean, leaving a settled mist beneath the high-altitude pedestrian tiers. He exhaled, the grey smoke blurring the Zen-like patterns etched into the company's alloy railing. There were no stacked billboards up here; the corporate elite didn't need to be sold. The polished walkways were empty, this early. The peak of New Veridia was a sleeping monster, ready to awake.

He didn't need to turn around to know he was no longer alone. The faint hiss of the glass doors sliding open behind him confirmed it.

Blackwell's voice was calm, carrying over the wind, "Are we ready, Miss Marlowe?" 

Elena Marlowe stood in the terrace entrance, framed by the cool glow of the holographic environmental data embedded in the glass of the door she had opened. Her lab coat stood out against the dark atrium behind her. A gust of cold wind whipped across the terrace, pushing her natural, shoulder-length hair across her face.

"Yes, Doctor Blackwell," she replied, her golden eyes darted constantly, tracking unseen calculations in the air as the reflection of the door's blue holograms reflected across their glassiness.

*Nervous*,  Blackwell thought, *and she should be*.  They were about to succeed in something  never before attempted in the history of modern quantum engineering.

"The Mnemosyne Engine is connected. Those city workers over at Main Fusion didn't like the request. But, you've got the three hundred and twenty terawatts," She said.

Blackwell raised the cigarette to his lips one last time, inhaling until the end burned close to the filter. He held the smoke in his lungs for another long second, then exhaled and dropped the butt over the edge of the railing.

The spent cigarette vanished into the fog. To Blackwell, the cloud cover was a necessary filter. It formed an opaque floor of vapor, swallowing the corroded foundations of the lower city. The Dead Circuits. Crushed remains of Oshima city. Decades ago, Takeda-Holden hadn't bothered to demolish Oshima, they poured the concrete for New Veridia directly on top of it. He knew what lived down there in the dark, buried beneath his feet: a tangle of failing systems, and the millions scurrying through the forgotten city. Noise beneath the sleeping monster.

Blackwell turned and walked past Elena, stepping through the sliding glass doors into the Takeda-Holden corporate atrium. The multi-tiered annex was an imposing expanse of sleek, featureless architecture. Sharp, geometric angles of polished concrete soared above and plunged below them. Blackwell walked towards the executive lift. Elena followed, her posture rigid as the steel elevator doors sealed them in. The high-speed descent was silent, dropping  dozens of floors down, past the bottom of the main atrium and into the Mnemosyne Lab.

When the doors parted, the air inside the lab moved with a low vibration. It was the terrifying reality of the city's reactor channeling into the floor beneath their feet.

Past the primary control consoles, the floor was manned by a mix of human technicians and the lab's impassive synthetics. They worked diligently checking the data on their screens. Blackwell approached the reinforced railing that encircled the center of the pit. Gripping the cold metal, he looked down.

Below him sat the Mnemosyne Engine—or at least, the very top of it. The staggering bulk of the machine was buried deep under the foundation and bedrock. What ascended through the facility's foundation, and protruded into this observation chamber was a cluster of stark white pillars of varying heights. They thrust sharply from the floor, terminating in angled points. A vertical display panel ran up one side of each pillar. Currently, every single screen was empty and black.

A heavy layer of white gas rolled across the engine pit, obscuring the white paneled floor beneath. It broke against the stark topology of the pillars like silent rapids before pouring over the edges of the floor like a waterfall, and recirculating. The air at the center of the array was bitter cold, a necessary extreme to keep the dormant core stabilized. The great engine was powered down, silent and waiting.

"A drop in the bucket for what we're asking it to unearth, Miss Marlowe," Blackwell said, his voice dropping into a gravelly rasp. He pulled a fresh cigarette from his coat pocket and slid it between his lips, though he didn't light it. 

Bracing his weight against the railing, he leaned forward, his reflection distorted in the reinforced glass. "The board wants better data compression. A smarter artificial intelligence to predict their margins."

A humorless breath fogged the glass. "What I'm building will pioneer a new era." He paused, listening to the quiet tick of the holographic monitors. "My singularity."

"Doctor Blackwell. Silas." Elena's voice interrupted, a note of warning creeping into her tone. "The board dialed in."

Above the primary consoles, a wall sized display flickered to life. The encrypted feed partitioned the screen into a grid of digital monitors. Each square displayed nothing but the featureless silhouette of a corporate executive.

"Perfect," Blackwell said, his lip curling into a sinister smile, "Just in time," his tone layered with passive sarcasm.

"The Takeda-Holden Corporation expects success today, Blackwell." a  voice echoed from the grid.

Blackwell didn't bother to look back at the screen or at Elena, keeping his gaze fixed on the dormant core below. "Very well, Miss Marlowe," he murmured. "Give them a show."

Elena didn't blink. She remained where she was, her posture shifting from subordinate to commander as she leaned into the microphone of the control room.  There was a quick succession of two tones over the speaker system. Technicians in white coats looked up from their consoles.

"All stations, ready for initialization." Elena's voice reverberated in a  crackle over the laboratory loudspeaker, almost robotic. "Initiate sequence."

The room erupted into a synchronized motion.

Below Blackwell, the engine responded. Dense fog pooling at the base of the pillars surged upward, flooding the pit as the heavy, iron-cast cooling valves released a torrent of thick gas. Then, one by one, the black screens on the sides of the white pillars displayed the boot sequence. They flickered at first, before stabilizing into flows of crisp white and blue text logs, falling down each pillar screen, illuminating the swirling fog in a spectral light.

The mechanical drone of the reactor shifted into a rhythmic pulse—a heavy vibration creeping through the concrete and into their bones. The very air in the lab felt thick with the sheer gravity of it.

Blackwell’s smile widened. He watched the data stream, entranced by the magnitude of the activation. He was calm, unaffected by the reverberating force of his creation. 

The Board watched from their remote sanctuaries, confident that distance would protect them. They didn't understand the physics. If the containment failed, the resulting rupture would swallow them as easily as the lab. 

Behind him, technicians on the floor exchanged nervous glances, shrinking back from the railing. They seemed unnerved by the sheer physical presence of the machine, but Blackwell remained unbothered. 

Elena stepped up to the railing, studying a datapad in her hand, her tense posture relaxing a fraction. "The initialization sequence is holding steady," she said, a rare note of satisfaction bleeding into her voice. She stepped closer to him, so only he could hear, "The containment calculations were flawless, Silas. You calculated activation. Congratulations."

"Of course they were," Blackwell said, the unlit cigarette bobbing between his lips.

On the executive display above the floor, the grid of silhouettes leaned forward. A metallic murmur of approval. Predatory interest. The Takeda-Holden Corporation was watching their investment come to life. Beside him, Elena stiffened under the executive gaze. To her, this was a performance.

"Doctor Blackwell, Ma'am." 

The hushed voice interrupted the moment. Iris, a Horizon Boundary Analyst, hurried up behind them, not looking up from the glowing datapad in her hands. She was in contrast to the rest of the room. Her hair was unkempt, defying the lab's strict dress code, and heavy gold hoops swung from her ears. She shoved her black-rimmed glasses up the bridge of her nose—a compulsive tic she exhibited whenever forced away from her screens. 

"Ma'am, local space-time—" Iris stammered in a frantic whisper, shooting a fearful glance at the Board's display before hiding behind her tablet. "It's—" She paused, her focus visibly fragmenting for a second, then continued. "a mass-displacement anomaly. A severe fluctuation in the event horizon."

Elena spun around. She cast a quick glance up at the Board's display—a fleeting dread that the subordinate could ruin the demonstration—before her piercing eyes snapped back to Iris.

The moment hung in the air. Blackwell observed the exchange in silence, recognizing the severity of the girl's intrusion without anxiety, curious to see how Elena would discipline the disruption.

Elena stepped into Iris's personal space. She didn't even look at the data.

"Negative," Elena said, her voice dropping into a scolding hiss through gritted teeth. "That is a predicted resonance."

Iris flinched, pulling the tablet tight against her chest. She swallowed hard, her eyes darting between the  machine and her supervisor. "Yes, ma'am. Of course. Predicted resonance."

"I need the telemetry!" Elena demanded, pivoting away.

At the adjacent console, a synthetic stood at attention. The designation *L-One-Two* (Laboratory 12) printed cleanly beneath its left eye. Its photoreceptive irises spun, emitting an eerie luminescence as they processed the influx. The construct shifted its gaze toward Blackwell, the motion disturbing the white fiber-optics rooted in its scalp. As the lenses locked onto his face, their rotation slowed into a semblance of admiration.

"Horizon stability confirmed," the machine reported.

Blackwell admired the synthetic. It existed solely for the objective, unburdened by human distractions. It was Elena's finest work. Of course, he knew her design was nothing compared to the singularity he had awakened.

"Fusion Control says the thermal limits are safe, Doctor," the synthetic stated, its voice flat. 

"Fusion Control doesn't understand the optics of my quantum singularity," Blackwell retorted.

A monotone voice echoed from the display grid above. "Satisfactory, Blackwell," a shadowed silhouette stated. "The Company commends your success."

Blackwell scoffed. "Humph."

Elena shot an apprehensive glance toward the panels. "What the Doctor means is, we are glad that today has been a success."

"Progress will be monitored," another executive replied. The connection severed.

"Mass Displacement Anomaly? That wasn't in the preliminary notes, Silas." Elena's true expression bled through as she looked at Blackwell, peering past his dark glasses.

Blackwell turned, giving her his undivided attention for the first time. "It's predicted resonance," he said, the corner of his mouth rising slightly.

Elena shook her head. Seeing Blackwell calm, she dropped the dangerous subject, "You know, it wouldn't hurt you to talk to the Board, " she turned, glancing back over the console floor behind them, "you are the lead."

Blackwell returned to the pillar readouts. The displacement anomaly was a necessary distraction. While Elena and the technicians panicked over the fluctuating event horizon, Blackwell watched the real experiment begin. He more than understood the Boundary Paradox—the way randomness and chaos grew unpredictably inside the structured borders of the universe he built within the horizon memory. Deep in the logs, a shadow process was quietly expanding. It was an inverted branch of the neural matrix. He caught the gaze of a nearby synthetic and gave a subtle nod. Its eyes flared, snapping its focus across the room to a second synthetic. Together they scrubbed the shadow process from the logs, silent, practiced, done before Elena could turn around.

"I don't like the Board," Blackwell said, keeping his gaze fixed on the engine.

"Funding, Silas," she urged. "Remember the funding."

"I don't like the Board," he repeated.

Elena exhaled, rubbing her temples. "And everybody knows it."
