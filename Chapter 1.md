The Mnemosyne Engine was built to tear a hole through the known universe and fill it with the mass of an anti-star.

Silas Blackwell took a drag from his cigarette, the ember flaring against the blues and ivory color of the early morning sky. A mop of white hair blew untamed in the wind, a contradiction to the sharp lines of his dark vest and tie. Over it all, he wore a lab coat that whipped around his legs in the cold air. He didn't button the coat. He liked the cold; it was the only thing at the top of the tower that hadn't been filtered by the Company first. 

He stood behind a pair of circular dark glasses, breathing a thin stream of smoke from his lips. From the wind-swept terrace of the Mnemosyne Lab, he watched his world.

Blackwell didn’t own any of it. He couldn’t buy a square foot of the platinum composite he was standing on. The tower belonged to the Takeda-Holden Corporation—the "Company"—a monument of corporate wealth. But as the Head of Synthetic Integration, Blackwell knew this building belonged to him in ways the Company would never comprehend.

The rain had cleared the air, leaving a settled mist beneath the high-altitude pedestrian tiers. He took another haul of his cigarette, the grey smoke skimming along the company's alloy railing. No stacked billboards cluttered the view at this altitude; nobody had to sell anything to the corporate elite. The walkways were empty this early—New Veridia was a sleeping monster, ready to awake.

He didn't need to turn around to know he was no longer alone. The faint hiss of the glass doors sliding open behind him confirmed it.

Blackwell's voice was calm, carrying over the wind. "Are we ready, Miss Marlowe?" 

Elena Marlowe stood in the terrace entrance, framed by the glow of the holographic environmental data embedded in the glass of the door she had opened. Her lab coat fell to her ankles. A gust of wind whipped the terrace, pushing her shoulder-length hair across the dark skin of her face.

"Yes, Doctor Blackwell," she replied. 

Elena didn't sound like herself. *Nervous*, Blackwell thought, *and she should be*. 

"The Mnemosyne Engine is connected. Those city workers over at Main Fusion didn't like the request. But, you've got the three hundred and twenty terawatts," she said.

Raising his cigarette to his lips one last time, the doctor inhaled until the end burned close to the filter. He held the smoke in his lungs for another long second, then exhaled and dropped the butt over the edge of the railing.

The spent cigarette vanished into the fog. To Blackwell, the cloud cover was a necessary filter. It formed an opaque floor between him and the corroded foundations of the lower city. 

The Dead Circuits. Crushed remains of Oshima city. Decades ago, Takeda-Holden hadn't bothered to demolish Oshima, they poured the concrete for New Veridia directly on top of it. He knew what lived down there in the dark, buried beneath his feet: a tangle of failing systems, and the millions scurrying through the forgotten city. Noise beneath the sleeping monster.

Blackwell turned and walked past Elena, stepping through the sliding glass doors into the corporate atrium. The multi-tiered annex was an expanse of sleek architecture. Sharp angles of polished concrete soared above and plunged below them. 

The building's tinted black glass refracted the rising sun, its rays shifting down forty floors below them. Blackwell walked towards the executive lift. Elena followed, her posture rigid as the steel elevator doors sealed them in. The high-speed descent was silent. He watched her from the corner of his eye. Her lips moved without a sound, her thumb tapped the edge of her datapad. Five years as his second-in-command, and she still treated the Board with too much reverence. He had picked her for her brilliance—though today, her nerves would make her useful. 

The lift dropped dozens of floors, past the bottom of the main atrium and into the Mnemosyne Lab. When the doors parted, the air inside the lab moved with a low vibration. It was the city's reactor, channeling into the floor beneath their feet.

Past the primary control consoles, the floor was manned by a mix of human technicians and the lab's impassive synthetics. They worked diligently checking the data on their screens. Blackwell approached the reinforced railing that encircled the center of the pit. The metal was cold. He gripped the rail.

Below him sat the Mnemosyne Engine—or at least, the very top of it. The staggering bulk of the machine was buried deep under the foundation and bedrock, farther down than anyone in this room had been allowed to see. Blackwell had stood at this rail on a hundred nights in the empty lab, and every time, he felt the cold breath of the engine rising from the pit. 

What ascended through the facility's foundation and protruded into this observation chamber was a cluster of white pillars of varying heights. They thrust sharply from the floor, terminating in angled points. To the technicians at their consoles, the pillars and the black screens running up each one were a high-end server array. To Blackwell, they were the crown of a cage, built to hold an anti-star.

Recirculated cooling gas rolled across the engine pit, hiding the floor beneath. It curled round the pillars and poured over the edges of the floor, stabilizing the dormant core. The great engine was powered down, silent and waiting.


"Main Fusion's supply is a drop in the bucket for what we're asking it to unearth, Miss Marlowe," Blackwell said, his voice dropping into a gravelly rasp. He pulled a fresh cigarette from his coat pocket and slid it between his lips, though he didn't light it. 

He braced himself against the railing and leaned forward, his reflection distorted in the safety glass. "The board wants better data compression. A smarter artificial intelligence to predict their margins."

A humorless breath fogged the glass. "What I'm building will pioneer a new era." He paused, listening to the quiet tick of the holographic monitors. "My singularity."

"Doctor Blackwell. Silas." Elena's voice interrupted, a note of warning creeping into her tone. "The board dialed in."

Above the primary consoles, a wall sized display flickered to life. The encrypted feed partitioned the screen into a grid of digital monitors. Each square displayed the featureless silhouette of a corporate executive.

"Perfect," Blackwell said, his lip curling. "Right on schedule."

"The Takeda-Holden Corporation expects success today, Blackwell," a voice echoed from the grid.

Blackwell didn't bother to look back at the screen or at Elena, keeping his gaze fixed on the dormant core. "Very well, Miss Marlowe," he murmured. "Give them a show."

Elena didn't blink. She remained where she was, her posture shifting from subordinate to commander as she leaned into the microphone of the control room.  There was a quick succession of two tones over the speaker system. Technicians in white coats looked up from their consoles.

"All stations, ready for initialization." Elena's voice reverberated in a  crackle over the laboratory loudspeaker, almost robotic. "Initiate sequence."

The room erupted into a synchronized motion.

Below Blackwell, the engine responded. Dense fog pooling at the base of the pillars surged upward, flooding the pit as the cooling valves released a torrent of thick gas. Then, one by one, the black screens on the sides of the pillars displayed the boot sequence. They flickered at first, before stabilizing into flows of crisp white and blue text logs, falling down each pillar screen, illuminating the swirling fog.

The mechanical drone of the reactor shifted into a rhythmic pulse, a vibration creeping through the concrete and into their bones. 

Blackwell’s smile widened. He watched the data streams, read every line as it fell. He was looking for one. The rhythm—the heartbeat of the engine—resonated through the bedrock, through the rail and into his palms. He tightened his grip and took in the magnitude of his creation.  

The Board watched from their remote offices, certain distance would keep them safe. They didn’t understand, if containment failed, the rupture would swallow them as easily as the lab. 

Behind him, the technicians exchanged nervous glances and stepped back from the railing. The machine unsettled them. 
Blackwell leaned in. 

Elena stepped up to the railing, studying a datapad in her hand, her tense posture relaxing a fraction. "The initialization sequence is holding steady," she said, a rare note of satisfaction bleeding into her voice. She stepped closer to him, so only he could hear, "The containment calculations were flawless, Silas. You calculated activation. Congratulations."

"Of course they were," Blackwell said, the unlit cigarette bobbing between his lips.

On the executive display above the floor, the grid of silhouettes leaned forward. A metallic murmur of approval. Predatory interest. The Takeda-Holden Corporation was watching their investment come to life. 

"Doctor Blackwell, Ma'am." 

The hushed voice interrupted the moment. Iris Ito, a Horizon Boundary Analyst, hurried up behind them, not looking up from the glowing datapad in her hands. She didn't belong with the rest of the room. Her hair was unkempt, defying the lab's strict dress code, and gold hoops swung from her ears. She compulsively shoved her black-rimmed glasses up the bridge of her nose—her focus snapping back to the glow of her screen. 

"Ma'am, local space-time—" Iris stammered in a frantic whisper, her eyes locked on her screen. "We have a change in the event horizon. A mass displacement anomaly. And—well actually, the mass-displacement is causing a—a localized gravitational shear." Her pressured speech fired rapidly, blurred together in a breathless recitation of awkward tones, "—and—well, you know—that could theoretically pull the primary containment of the horizon's gravity field out of phase—and if we don't recalculate, then the—" 

She trailed off, finally looking up from her screen. 

Elena was staring at her, eyes piercing. "Negative," she said, her voice dropping into a scolding hiss through gritted teeth.

She cast a quick glance up at the Board's display, and then to Blackwell—an expression of fleeting dread that the subordinate could ruin the demonstration—before her piercing eyes snapped back to Iris.

Blackwell side-eyed the exchange. The girl's numbers were good. He didn't need to see her screen to know it.

Stepping into Iris's personal space, she didn't look at the data. "That is a predicted resonance. Go recalculate the gravity phase changes."

Iris flinched, pulling the tablet tight against her chest. "Yes, ma'am. Of course. Predicted resonance."

Elena had told his lie for him. She didn't even know she had.

"I need the tracking data!" Elena demanded, pivoting away.

At the adjacent console, a synthetic stood at attention. The designation *L-One-Two* (Laboratory 12) printed cleanly beneath its left eye. Its photoreceptive irises spun, emitting a liquid crystal luminescence as they processed the influx. The construct shifted its gaze toward Blackwell, the motion disturbing the shining fiber-optics rooted in its scalp. As the lenses locked onto his face, their rotation slowed into a semblance of admiration.

"Horizon stability confirmed," the machine reported.

Blackwell admired the synthetic. It existed solely for the objective, unburdened by human distractions. It was Elena's finest work. Of course, he knew her design was nothing compared to the singularity he had awakened.

"Fusion Control says the thermal limits are safe, Doctor," the synthetic stated, its voice flat. 

"Fusion Control doesn't understand the optics of my quantum singularity," Blackwell retorted.

A monotone voice echoed from the display grid above. "Satisfactory, Blackwell," a shadowed silhouette stated. "The Company commends your success."

Blackwell scoffed. "Humph."

Elena glance toward the panels. "What the Doctor means is, we are glad that today has been a success."

"Progress will be monitored," another executive replied. The connection severed.

"Mass Displacement Anomaly? That wasn't in the preliminary notes, Silas." Elena's true expression bled through as she looked at Blackwell, peering past his dark glasses.

Blackwell turned, giving her his undivided attention for the first time.  She was looking for his eyes behind the dark lens of his glasses. He let her. 
"It's predicted resonance," he said, the corner of his mouth rising.

Elena shook her head. He watched her decide to believe him. "You know, it wouldn't hurt you to talk to the Board." She turned, glancing back over the console floor behind them. "You are the lead."

The cigarette was still unlit between his lips. He wanted a drag. 

Blackwell returned to the pillar readouts. A displacement anomaly was a perfect distraction. While Elena and the technicians monitored the event horizon, Blackwell observed the real experiment begin. 

Listed in the logs, record of a shadow process, quietly expanding in the core. 

His teeth closed on the filter of the unlit cigarette. *There it was.*

Blackwell caught the gaze of a nearby synthetic and gave a subtle nod. Its eyes flared, snapping its focus to a second synthetic on the far side of the room. Together they scrubbed the shadow process from the logs, silent, practiced, done before Elena could turn around.

"I don't like the Board," Blackwell said, keeping his gaze fixed on the engine.

"Funding, Silas," she urged. "Remember the funding."

"I don't like the Board," he repeated.

Elena exhaled, rubbing her temples. "And everybody knows it."
