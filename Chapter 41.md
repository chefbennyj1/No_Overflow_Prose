Frank Harlan walked down the faded seafoam hallway of Oshima Civic Care. The blue and yellow checkered linoleum stuck to his shoes. Overhead, the fluorescents burned steady. In all the times he had walked this corridor behind Victor, he had never seen them hold.

He stopped at the front desk and picked up the chained stylus. The last line in the visitor log was in Victor's hand, the letters slanted hard to the right. Harlan set the stylus under it.

He looked up at the nurse behind the desk. "Frank Harlan, for Arthur Graves."

The nurse looked past his shoulder, down the empty corridor. Then she turned her screen toward him before he finished signing. Three numbers sat under the balance.

Harlan paid all three.

"It comes due again on the first," the nurse said.

"Then I'll see you on the first."

He continued down the corridor to the green wooden door. The fake blue flowers sat in their cheap vase beside the frame, collecting dust. There were seven. He had counted them through every visit Victor ever made, and he had never seen the other side of the door.

Jackie had the boxes stacked by the front door at home. He had told her he had one stop to make.

Harlan straightened his tie and pushed the door open.

The room was smaller than the hallway had let him picture. An old man sat in a patched corduroy armchair, his white hair parted to the side, his eyes going from the cracked window to the doorway and back.

"Close it," Arthur whispered. His fingers gripped the armrests. "You gotta keep an eye—" He pointed a trembling finger at the window. "There is an expectation here. The Madame don't just trust anyone, ya know."

Harlan had seen the Madame lying in the snow on the plaza, her head turned too far to one side. He closed the door.

"Mr. Graves," he said. "My name's Frank. I'm a friend of Victor's."

"Victor."

The finger came down. Arthur's hand settled on the armrest and lay open there. He turned from the window, and focus came into his clouded eyes. He looked Harlan over, shoes to tie.

"You know my Victor?"

"Yes, sir. A long time."

"Oh, you know my Victor, then." The tremble had gone out of his voice. "He's a good boy. Always doin' the right thing." Arthur looked down at his own hands. "Didn't learn that from me."

Harlan stood with his back against the door. He had held Victor on the frost and gone over his coat for a wound, and there had been nothing to press on. His hands had not known what to do. He put them in his pockets.

"He sits in that chair." Arthur nodded at the plastic chair against the wall. "One minute. Then he's up and gone. He thinks I can't tell how long a minute is."

"He's got a lot of work, Mr. Graves."

"He's got a father he can't look at." Arthur said it without heat. "That's all right. I gave him cause." He raised his eyes to Harlan. "He comin' today?"

"Not today."

Arthur nodded. He leaned forward in the armchair, and his voice dropped. "Then you tell him somethin' for me. You tell him his father said—"

His head snapped toward the window. His fingers dug back into the corduroy. When he looked at Harlan again, the focus had gone out of his eyes.

"You!" Arthur's lip curled. "You smell like a cop."

Harlan waited for the rest of the sentence. It didn't come.

"No, Arthur," he said. "Not today."

"Then you should be watchin' the window. You wanna get us strung up?"

"Alright, Arthur." Harlan pulled the plastic chair across the room and sat down in front of the glass. The seat was low for him, and his knees rode high. "I'll watch the window."

Across the gap between the tenements, the rusted track ran empty to the mouth of the subway tunnel. A column of smoke stood behind the rooftops, thin now, leaning with the wind. Below the window, the streetlights burned orange and didn't flicker.

Harlan put his hands on his knees.

Behind him, the armchair creaked. Arthur's breathing was loud in the small room, a dry pull and a long release.

A mag-train came out from between the buildings. It crawled along the track at a walking pace, half its windows dark, and Harlan watched the passenger cars shudder past until the red marker on the rear went into the tunnel.

"He buys his coats out of a bin," Harlan said. He kept his eyes on the track. "Every winter I tell him. Get one off the rack, Vic. One that fits. He looks at me like I asked him to burn money."

Arthur laughed, one dry breath through his nose. "Nothin' wrong with a bin. A coat's a coat." The armchair creaked again. "Boy knows what a thing costs. He learned that one from me."

"Yes, sir," Harlan said. "He did."

A second train went by.

By now Victor was always back out in the hall, collar up, already walking.

Harlan stayed in the chair.

A third train passed. Then a fourth, slower than the others.

"They're running again, Arthur," he said. "The trains."

Nothing came back.

Harlan listened for the dry pull of the old man's breath. The room had gone quiet enough to hear a cart rolling somewhere down the corridor.

He looked back.

Arthur sat in the corduroy armchair with his face toward the glass. His hands lay open on the armrests. His eyes had stopped going to the door.

Harlan didn't get up. He turned back around in the plastic chair and faced the window.

Another train came out from between the buildings. He watched it the whole way to the tunnel.
