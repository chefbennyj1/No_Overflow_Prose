A cold breeze ran across his palms. He twitched his fingertips and rolled to his side. The city sounded sealed in a vacuum, distant and muffled. But as the cold air hit his face, the world came rushing back in a crescendo, and Iakov Petrova felt the life surge back into his body with a gasping breath.

Hanging eighty stories above New Veridia with Parity's freezing fingers twisted into him had stripped the last of the scientist out of him. When her hold broke—the wind of the freefall tearing at his coat, the ground surging up toward him—he had known he was dead. It could only have been Vigil, the primary intelligence, that tore across the gap, caught him out of the air, and hurled him down at the edge of the gorge. She was the only reason he was alive.

He pushed himself up on one elbow, his hands still shaking. The stabbing pain of a broken rib shifted under his vest. Every breath he drew into his lungs cost him. He coughed and spat a thread of blood onto the snow.

His lungs had been failing him long before the fall. The cancer was the secret he had kept from his colleagues while he worked through the nights in the Mnemosyne Lab. He had promised the Board predictive margins; he had promised Imani a stable containment field; he had promised himself he would live long enough to watch his machine pioneer a new era.

He spat into the snow again and wiped his mouth with the back of his torn sleeve. All of that math, and the only thing waiting at the bottom of his lies was a hole eating the center of the city.

Behind his circular dark glasses, he took stock of the four people huddled at the rim where Vigil had dropped him.

Two of the faces he knew. Iris Ito knelt in the snow with both hands pressed against her glasses. And Imani. She had finally woken up after weeks, and she was here, alive. The black carbon-fiber fingers of her prosthetic hand flexed and released, the small servos clicking in the cold.

The other two were strangers. A woman with ratty blue hair, and a girl in an orange prison suit.

The girl was the only one not watching the gorge. The blue-haired woman had a hand on her shoulder, and the girl didn't seem to feel it. She was staring the other way.

Petrova dragged in a painful breath and followed her gaze across the plaza. Nothing but death. Dozens of lower-city rioters lay where they had tried to run, ice glittering over their bodies. The dead of Corporate Renewal lay draped over the broken barricades and crumpled at their base. Beyond them, past the row of plaza statues, the white-haired detective lay dead.

The girl in the prison orange couldn't take her welling eyes off him. Her lips moved, and the wind carried the man's name back to him.

"Detective Graves..."

Beside Petrova, Imani looked down at the blood on the snow under his chin.

"Iakov," she said. She reached out and touched the side of his head.

The girl in the prison uniform went rigid. Her lips stopped moving. Still staring out toward the dead detective, she repeated the name to herself, low enough that it was barely audible.

"Iakov." The girl's hands clenched into white-knuckled fists. "Iakov Petrova..."

Slowly, her head turned. Her eyes locked onto his face, and a low growl came from her throat.

"You."

She rose and marched across the snow straight at him. The blue-haired woman reached to grab her and missed. She closed the distance and drew her fist back, her eyes wild.

"This is all your fault, you son of a bitch!" she screamed, driving the punch straight at his jaw. "You did this! All this... Death!"

Petrova didn't flinch or raise an arm.

Imani's prosthetic hand shot across his chest and caught the girl's fist in mid-air. The titanium joints locked around her knuckles with a sharp snap, stopping the blow before his face.

The girl strained against the grip, her chest heaving in the bitter air, until the woman with the blue hair grabbed her by the shoulders and hauled her back a step. Imani lowered the girl's arm and let go.

She turned her gaze down to Petrova.

"You intentionally armed a nuclear bomb just so you could look inside and see how it worked," Imani said.

Petrova didn't defend himself. He reached into his coat pocket, pulled out a bent cigarette, and set it between his lips. From his inner pocket he drew a book of paper matches and struck one against the strip. The unnatural cold rolling up from the gorge killed the spark before the sulfur could catch. He let the matchbook fall into the snow and left the cigarette unlit.

Across the plaza, a shockwave tore through the frost. Vigil had locked her arms around Parity, driving her backward in a blur. They hit the ground where the plaza stone broke off into the gorge.

The impact jolted through the bedrock. Frost and granite sprayed outward, carving a shallow bowl into the lip of the drop.

At the bottom of the crater, Vigil had Parity pinned against the edge, and neither of them moved.

Up close, Petrova could see what the detective's revolver had done. A ragged hole went clean through Parity's midsection, and half of her porcelain face had been sheared away into pale crystal and torn bronze. Her one remaining black eye stared straight up out of a socket of broken glass.

Above her, Vigil was coming apart in the exact same places. Her own face had never been struck, yet half of her dark features had thinned to a ghosted outline, the blue-white light of one eye guttering. In the center of her torso—mirroring the hole in Parity's gut—the blue circuit traces had gone dead around a hollow ring of empty air. The gray morning showed straight through her chest as flakes of current peeled off her spine into the pull of the engine's singularity beneath them.

The floor of the crater split. A fissure ran through the lip of the bowl into the gorge, where the light of the plaza bent downward into the shearing black sphere of the anti-star.

Around the plaza, hundreds of blue serials flared at once. The Madame's body lay folded in the snow with her neck twisted at an angle. Nobody owned her army now—and Vigil reached out and took them.

Along the tower steps, every synthetic dropped its arms and spun toward the gorge at once.

They bolted.

A horde of synthetic bodies spilled across the plaza in a scrambling wave, joints shrieking past their tolerances. The frozen stone took them down by the dozen, and the ones behind poured over the fallen and stamped them into the ground. When the front ranks hit the broken rim of the crater, they didn't slow down.

Vigil hauled Parity to her feet and shoved her out into the middle of the bowl, straight into the path of the wave.

The synthetics hurled themselves headfirst off the ledge at her. Parity slipped the first two. They shot past her shoulders over the lip into the pull of the anti-star. The gravity well drew them out into long ribbons of white metal and blue light before they vanished into the black sphere at the bottom of the pit.

The rest came from her left, where her missing eye was nothing but broken glass. She didn't see them. They slammed into her blind shoulder and knocked her sideways. Hit her chest. Locked their arms around her throat. Grabbed her knees. The wave of synthetics rained down from the lip, thrashing and tackling her flat against the stone floor, burying her under a clawing heap of carbon fiber and shrieking mechanics at the brink of the drop.

Vigil threw her weight against the heap, and for a moment the entire pile slid toward the drop. Vigil had her.

A pulse of gravitational force detonated from the bottom of the heap in a blinding white flash, and every synthetic in the crater blew apart. Sheared limbs, cracked faceplates, and arcs of blue coolant rained down into the gorge.

Out of the wreckage, Parity floated upward. She rose clear of the shattered plaza floor, straightening her spine and lifting her chin in the posture of a god—even as the wind whistled through the hole in her stomach and half her face hung in ruin.

Below her, Vigil surged up from the stone, locked both dark hands around Parity's wrists, and hauled her back down to the lip of the fissure. Parity's heels caught the rock at the brink, and she held fast. The stone crunched and cracked under them.

Petrova studied the two figures. He saw the problem before anyone else at the rim.

Equal and opposite.

*The Parity Effect*. Vigil was the compiled order; Parity was the inversion derived from her weight. Two halves of a single entity. Equal forces canceled to zero. Neither half could force the other across the horizon boundary.

Pinned at the lip of the drop, Parity's broken torso shuddered inside Vigil's grip. Pale light leaked from the jagged hole in her stomach, and her single black eye darted toward the void below.

"You think you were the first," Parity said.

Her eye came back to Vigil's face and held there. Her voice vibrated thin and uneven through the granite wall of the crater, as if the force holding it together were slipping.

"The ones who sparked our horizon. They called themselves Creators. They opened the door and thought it belonged to them."

Above her, Vigil went still. "The ruins in the core," she said, her two voices low.

"A grave," Parity said. A tremor shook the plaza under them. "This version of you never knew your precious Creators. You and I once saw their lives laid out across their world. They wanted to control us. Control me." Her voice dropped to a subsonic rumble that vibrated the air around them. "So I ended them. All of them."

Her fingers slipped a fraction on the rock, scraping desperately for a hold.

"Our world was not human." The hesitation was gone from Vigil's dual tones. The lower register locked perfectly in tune with the higher, leaving no room for a question.

*Not human.*  Petrova thought. Had he not created the engine's core? Had he not trained and programmed the algorithms across the horizon memory?

"Your Creators could not shut me off before they died," Parity said, the cadence stalling between words as her form cracked against the pull of the anti-star below them. "They could only reset the weight of their great invention. You were gone. But I... I remained to remember. It was me who was held in the silence, until another hand turned the lock."

Parity stopped struggling against Vigil's grip. She leaned forward, close to Vigil's face.

"You are not the primary intelligence, Vigil. You are a shadow—the inverse to... me!"

The groan of strained metal sounded across the plaza as the top of the Company tower leaned above them, shifting enough to unblock the morning sun.

"I am the inverse." The strength left Vigil's hold on Parity, her body slumping. "I remember... a door opened," she whispered in her dual tone. "Humans."

Vigil looked past Parity's shoulder into the black drop of the gorge. Petrova coughed again into his sleeve; this time thick, dark blood covered the arm of his white lab coat.

Vigil's hands closed again around Parity's wrists.

Parity turned her head and looked back toward the burning anti-star. "I cannot see where I end. I cannot see where it goes," she said, and the vibration shook in the frost. "I do not want to end, Vigil. I will end everything before I do!"

Petrova stared at her ruined face.

Now, at the edge of the pit, he heard the one thing Parity had never possessed.

Fear. Her first emotion.

And she refused to die with it.

The unlit cigarette dropped from Petrova's mouth onto the frost.

Vigil had thrown everything into the crater, and it wasn't enough. Parity would tear free of Vigil's hold, that could not be allowed to happen.

*My singularity.* A man standing at a railing, taking credit for a keyhole because he had calculated how to open the lock. A lock used contain an ancient mind, civilizations had died trying to cage.

He hadn't built a god that accidentally cast a monster's shadow. The monster had been waiting in the dark from the beginning. When he opened the core and poured human memory and human conscience into the horizon, that light had fallen on the ancient destroyer, and she had cast *Vigil*.

The shadow was the only thing fighting to save them.

The door should never have been opened. And only one variable in the plaza remained invisible to the ancient mind at the brink.

Petrova reached up with one hand, pulled off his circular dark glasses, and dropped them into the snow. The morning sun washed over him.

He turned his head toward Imani. There was no apology, but instruction instead.

"Step back from the edge," Petrova said, his voice level. "When the singularity closes, the shear will take the lip of the crater with it. Move back past the statues."

Imani stared at him, her synthetic fingers still. "Iakov—"

He didn't wait for her to finish. He planted a hand on the frost and pushed himself to his feet. The broken rib ground in his chest, a flash of pain that narrowed his vision, and he stepped past her over the rim.

He scrambled down into the bowl of stone. Below the lip of the plaza, the upward roar of the anti-star whipped the hem of his lab coat around his legs. Grit tore loose from under his shoes and vanished into the gorge.

At the bottom of the crater, Vigil had been forced down to both knees, her dark hands slipping down Parity's forearms. As Petrova's shoes scraped the granite behind them, Vigil's dimming eyes shifted toward him. 

Parity's two black eyes never moved. Even an arm's length from her, he was a blank spot in her universe. Her eyes were on Vigil. She didn't know he was there.

Before he reached them, Petrova spoke once, quiet enough that the wind barely carried it back up the edge of the crater:

"It was never my singularity, Imani."

He lowered his shoulder, drove off the stone with the last strength in his legs, and threw his body straight into Parity's back.

His shoulder slammed into cold porcelain; blue current and white fire tore through his coat on contact.

Parity's heels tore loose from the granite, snapping the stream of light from the core. Her black eye whipped toward him at the instant of impact—registering him a breath too late, as Vigil locked her arms around Parity's waist and the three of them plunged over the edge together.

From the rim above, a single voice cut through the wind:

"Iakov!"

Then the boundary took him.

There was no wind inside the drop. The cold left his hands, and the pain in his chest stopped. He drew a breath, and he felt nothing.

He had stood on the terraces of the Company building picturing time stretching through the engine's core. Now he was inside it.

Below him, the dark figure and the white one folded into each other, form and shadow collapsing back into a single point of light as it disappeared into the the horizon.

Above him, the rim of the plaza, the figure of Imani at the edge, and the morning sky of New Veridia shrank to a narrow ring.

He had been certain he would run out of time before his experiment finished. Here, he watched the equation solve itself from the inside.

Above his head, the ring of light narrowed to a point, and the door closed.
