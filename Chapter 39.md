A cold breeze ran across his palms. He twitched his finger tips, and rolled to his side. His world sounded sealed  in a vacuum. Distant, and muffled. But, as the cool breeze hit his face, his world came rushing back in a crescendo, and Silas Blackwell felt the life surge back into his body with a gasping breath.

Hanging eighty stories above New Veridia with Parity's freezing fingers twisted into him, had stripped the last of the scientist out of him. When Parity's hold broke, the freefall, the wind tearing at his coat and the street rushing up to meet him, he had known he was dead. What could have only been Vigil, the primary intelligence, tore across the gap, caught him out of the air, and hurled him down into the ground at the edge of the gorge. It was the only reason he was alive.

He pushed himself up on one elbow on the frosted ground, his hands still shaking. The stabbing pain of a broken rib shifted under his vest. Every breath he drew into his lungs cost him. He coughed and spat a thread of blood onto the snow.

His lungs had been failing him long before the fall. That was the secret he had kept locked in his medical files while he worked through the nights in the Mnemosyne Lab. He had promised the Board predictive margins; he had promised Elena a stable containment field; he had promised himself he would live long enough to watch his machine pioneer a new era.

He spat into the snow again and wiped his mouth with the back of his torn sleeve. All of that math, and the only thing waiting at the bottom of his lies was a hole eating the center of the city.

Behind his circular dark glasses, he took stock of the four people huddled at the rim where Vigil had dropped him.

Two of the faces he knew. Iris Ito knelt in the snow with both hands pressed against her cracked black-rimmed glasses. And Elena. She had finally woken up after weeks, and she was here, alive. The black carbon-fiber fingers of her prosthetic hand flexed and released, the small servos clicking in the cold.

The other two were strangers. A tall woman in a leather jacket, with ratty blue hair. The other with jagged hair, in an orange prison suit.
Blackwell rasped a painful breath in and stared out across the plaza.

Across the stone, dozens of lower-city rioters lay frozen where they had tried to run, locked in ice beside the broken barricades and the bodies of Corporate Renewal. Beyond the carnage, near the overturned cruiser, the white-haired detective lay dead.

Beside Blackwell, Elena looked down at the blood on the snow under his chin.

"Silas," she said, her voice tight.

The girl in the prison trousers went rigid. Still staring out toward the dead detective, she spoke the name to herself, low enough that it barely carried across the wind.

"Silas Blackwell..."

Slowly, her head turned. Her eyes locked onto his face, and a low growl came from her throat.

"You."

She rose and marched across the snow straight at him. The blue-haired woman reached to grab her and missed. She closed the distance in three strides, the girl drew her fist back, her eyes wild.

"This is all your fault, you son of a bitch!" she screamed, driving the punch straight at his jaw. "You did this! All this death!"

Blackwell didn't flinch or raise an arm.

An inch from his glasses, Elena's prosthetic hand shot across his chest and caught the girl's fist in mid-air. The titanium joints locked around her knuckles with a sharp snap, stopping the blow cold.

The girl strained against the grip, her chest heaving in the bitter air, until the woman in the leather jacket grabbed her by the shoulders and hauled her back a step. Elena lowered the girl's arm and let go.

She turned her gaze down to Blackwell.

"You intentionally armed a nuclear bomb just so you could admire the clockwork inside it," Elena said.

Blackwell didn't defend himself. He reached into his coat pocket, pulled out a bent cigarette, and set it between his lips. From his inner pocket he drew a book of paper matches and struck one against the strip. The unnatural cold rolling up from the gorge killed the spark before the sulfur could catch. He let the matchbook fall into the snow and left the cigarette unlit.

Across the plaza, a shockwave tore through the frost. Vigil had locked her arms around Parity, driving her backward in a low blur. They hit the ground where the plaza stone broke off into the gorge.

The impact jolted through the bedrock into his knees. Frost and granite sprayed outward, carving a shallow bowl into the lip of the drop.

At the bottom of the crater, Vigil had Parity pinned against the edge.

Up close, Blackwell could see what the detective's revolver had done. A ragged hole went clean through Parity's midsection, and half of her porcelain face had been sheared away into pale crystal and torn bronze wire. Her one remaining black eye stared straight up out of a socket of broken glass.

Above her, Vigil was coming apart in the exact same places. Her own face had never been struck, yet half of her dark features had thinned to a ghosted outline, the white light of one eye guttering and dim. In the center of her torso—mirroring the crater in Parity's gut—the blue circuit traces had gone dead around a hollow ring of empty air, the gray morning showing straight through her chest as flakes of current peeled off her spine into the pull of the engine's singularity beneath them.

The floor of the crater split. A fissure ran through the lip of the bowl into the gorge, where the light of the plaza bent downward into the rising black sphere of the anti-star. Grit slid over the edge into nothing.

Vigil braced her heels against the granite and dragged Parity toward the drop. Parity's fingers dug into the stone, and neither of them moved.

Around the plaza, hundreds of blue serials flared at once.

The Madame's body lay folded in the snow with her neck twisted at an angle. Nobody owned her army now—and Vigil reached out and took them.

Along the tower steps, every synthetic dropped its arms and spun toward the gorge. They were Elena's frames, her clean geometry, and as Vigil's signal hit their cores, every speed limiter tore open at once.

They didn't march. They bolted.

Hundreds of synthetic bodies sprinted across the plaza in a rabid, scrambling wave, joints shrieking past their mechanical tolerances, limbs pistoning so hard that plastic casing cracked off their calves. Those in the front ranks dropped to all fours to claw across the slick frost for traction, and when they hit the broken rim of the crater, they didn't slow down. They hurled themselves headfirst off the ledge at Parity.

Parity wrenched one arm free of Vigil's grip and struck the first two out of the air, shattering their skulls into white shrapnel. She twisted her torso aside as a dozen more dove past her shoulders.

The synthetics that missed her shot straight over the lip into the open pull of the anti-star.

Blackwell watched the gravity well catch them in mid-air. The tidal shear took their boots before their heads, drawing their titanium skeletons and plastic skin out into long, thin ribbons of white metal and blue light—stretching them twenty feet down into the dark before they snapped into the black sphere at the bottom of the gorge.

Still the horde poured over the rim without stopping, leaping in from every side of the bowl at once.

Parity shattered three frames diving from her right, her porcelain fist punching clean through their chests—and then four more launched themselves from her ruined left side, where her missing eye was nothing but broken glass.

She didn't see them come.

The first synthetic slammed into her blind shoulder and knocked her sideways. The second hit her chest. The third locked its arms around her throat, and the fourth took her at the knees. More bodies rained down from the lip behind them—striking her again, and again, and again—until thirty thrashing frames tackled her flat against the stone floor, burying her five deep under a clawing heap of carbon fiber and shrieking servos at the very brink of the abyss.

For a breath, the entire pile surged toward the drop. Vigil had her.

Then a blinding pulse of white gravitational force detonated from the bottom of the heap.

Every synthetic in the crater blew apart in a single instant. Sheared limbs, cracked faceplates, and arcs of blue coolant blasted into the air and rained down into the gorge.

Out of the wreckage, Parity floated upward. She rose a foot above the shattered stone, straightening her spine and lifting her chin in the cold, untouchable posture of a god—even as the wind whistled through the crater in her stomach and half her face hung in ruin.

Below her, Vigil surged up from the stone, locked both dark hands around Parity's wrists, and hauled her back down to the lip of the fissure. Parity's heels caught the rock at the brink, holding her ground half a foot above the drop.

Vigil drove her weight forward, her edges fraying into static as she strained toward the abyss, and Parity held fast. The stone groaned under them, refusing to give.

Blackwell studied the two figures through his circular dark glasses. He saw the problem before anyone else at the rim.

Equal and opposite. Perfect symmetry.

He had written the proof himself three years before Takeda-Holden poured the concrete: *The Parity Effect*. Vigil was the compiled order; Parity was the inversion derived from her weight. Two halves of a single closed system. Inside a closed equation, equal forces canceled to zero. Even with Elena's army spent, neither half could force the other across the boundary.

Breaking a symmetry required a hand from outside the equation.

Hovering at the lip of the drop, Parity's single black eye stayed fixed on Vigil's burning white one.

"You think you were the first," Parity said.

Her remaining lips didn't move. The vibration came up through the granite wall of the crater, faint enough that only those at the rim could hear it over the wind.

"The ones who left the stone in the dark believed they built me too," Parity said. "They called themselves creators. They opened the door and thought the room belonged to them."

Above her, Vigil went still. "The ruins in the core," she said, her two voices low. "They were not the engine."

"They were a grave," Parity said. "Them, I could see. Every life across their worlds, from first breath to last. I ended them. All of them."

Her fingers slipped a fraction in the frost, then locked again.

"They could not shut me off before they died," she said. "They could only reset the weight. Leave the silence to hold me until another hand turned the lock."

A tremor ran through the stone under Blackwell's elbow, vibrating straight out of Parity's chest.

The flat certainty that had rung through the tower glass was gone. The cadence broke apart, stalling between syllables as her calculations failed to close.

"Then a door opened," Parity whispered through the stone. "And in this world, there are things I cannot see."

Her single black eye widened in its socket of shattered glass. She was looking past Vigil's shoulder into the black drop of the gorge, where her own world line ended.

"I cannot see where it goes," she said, and the vibration shook in the frost. "I do not want to end."

Blackwell stared down at her ruined face.

For four weeks he had listened to her through safety glass, comms, and eighty-story window panes. Now, at the edge of the pit, he heard the one thing she had never possessed.

Fear. Her first emotion, and her last.

Above the crater, the flakes of snow that had hung suspended in the air gave way all at once, falling normally again across the shattered synthetic frames and the torn stone.

The unlit cigarette dropped from Blackwell's mouth onto the frost.

He had built nothing.

*My singularity.* A man standing at a railing, taking credit for a keyhole because he had calculated the torque on the lock. The monoliths drifting in the horizon memory were tombstones from the last civilization brilliant enough to tear the hole open. Every species that found the equation believed they were pioneering a new era, and the last one hadn't even left a name behind.

The door should never have been opened.

Blackwell reached up with one hand, pulled off his circular dark glasses, and dropped them into the snow. The glare off the frost stung his bare eyes. He didn't blink.

He turned his head toward Elena. A man who could not apologize gave procedure instead.

"Step back from the edge," Blackwell said, his voice level. "When the mass clears the boundary, the shear will take the lip of the crater with it. Move them back ten meters."

Elena stared at him, her synthetic fingers still. "Silas—"

He didn't wait for her to finish. He planted a hand on the frost and pushed himself to his feet. The broken rib ground together in his chest, a flash of pain that narrowed his vision, and he stepped past her over the rim.

He climbed down into the bowl of stone.

Ten feet below the plaza level, the pull of the anti-star dragged at the hem of his lab coat. Loose grit slid out from under his shoes and vanished into the gorge.

At the bottom of the crater, Vigil and Parity remained locked at the brink, trembling under the strain of a balanced equation. As Blackwell's shoes scraped the granite behind them, Vigil's white eyes shifted toward him. She didn't tell him to stop.

Parity's single black eye never moved. Even an arm's length from her ruined face, he was a blank spot in her universe. She didn't know he was standing behind her.

Before he reached them, Blackwell spoke once, quiet enough that the wind barely carried it back up the stone:

"It was never my singularity, Elena."

For four weeks he had kept his work behind safety glass and scrubbed logs. Now he reached out and laid one bare palm against the cold porcelain of Parity's back, and the other against the flaking dark of Vigil's shoulder.

Ice bit into the skin of his left hand; blue current burned the skin of his right.

*Vigil is the form. Parity is the shadow it casts. You cannot keep one without the other.*

Blackwell braced his feet in the stone, leaned his weight into his broken chest, and shoved.

The outside variable tipped the scale.

Parity's fingers tore loose from the granite. Her black eye snapped toward him at the instant of contact—registering the doctor half a second too late, as the three of them went over the edge together.

From the rim above, a single voice cut through the wind:

"Silas!"

Then the boundary took him.

There was no wind inside the drop. The cold left his hands, and the pain in his chest stopped.

Four weeks ago, standing on the terrace with a cigarette between his fingers, Silas Blackwell had pictured time stretching through the engine's core. Now he was inside it.

Below him, the dark figure and the white one folded into each other, form and shadow collapsing back into a single point of light as the horizon reclaimed its weight.

Above him, the rim of the plaza, the figure of Elena at the edge, and the morning sky of New Veridia shrank to a narrow ring.

The man who had been dying by inches, certain he would run out of time before his experiment finished, watched the equation solve itself from the inside.

Above his head, the ring of light narrowed to a point, and the door closed.
