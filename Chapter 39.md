A cold breeze ran across his palms. He twitched his fingertips and rolled to his side. His world sounded sealed in a vacuum, distant and muffled. But as the breeze hit his face, the world came rushing back in a crescendo, and Silas Blackwell felt the life surge back into his body with a gasping breath.

Hanging eighty stories above New Veridia with Parity's freezing fingers twisted into him had stripped the last of the scientist out of him. When her hold broke—the wind of the freefall tearing at his coat, the ground surging up toward him—he had known he was dead. It could only have been Vigil, the primary intelligence, that tore across the gap, caught him out of the air, and hurled him down at the edge of the gorge. She was the only reason he was alive.

He pushed himself up on one elbow, his hands still shaking. The stabbing pain of a broken rib shifted under his vest. Every breath he drew into his lungs cost him. He coughed and spat a thread of blood onto the snow.

His lungs had been failing him long before the fall. The cancer was the secret he had kept from his colleagues while he worked through the nights in the Mnemosyne Lab. He had promised the Board predictive margins; he had promised Elena a stable containment field; he had promised himself he would live long enough to watch his machine pioneer a new era.

He spat into the snow again and wiped his mouth with the back of his torn sleeve. All of that math, and the only thing waiting at the bottom of his lies was a hole eating the center of the city.

Behind his circular dark glasses, he took stock of the four people huddled at the rim where Vigil had dropped him.

Two of the faces he knew. Iris Ito knelt in the snow with both hands pressed against her glasses. And Elena. She had finally woken up after weeks, and she was here, alive. The black carbon-fiber fingers of her prosthetic hand flexed and released, the small servos clicking in the cold.

The other two were strangers. A tall woman in a leather jacket with ratty blue hair, and a girl in an orange prison suit.

The girl was the only one not watching the gorge. The blue-haired woman had a hand on her shoulder, and the girl didn't seem to feel it. She was staring the other way.

Blackwell dragged in a painful breath and followed her gaze across the plaza. Nothing but death. Dozens of lower-city rioters lay where they had tried to run, ice glittering over their bodies. The dead of Corporate Renewal lay draped over the broken barricades and crumpled at their base. Beyond them, past the row of plaza statues, the white-haired detective lay dead.

The girl in the prison orange couldn't take her welling eyes off him. Her lips moved, and the wind carried the man's name back to him.

"Detective Graves..."

A convict grieving a policeman. Even in the ruin of the plaza, the sight was strange.

Beside Blackwell, Elena looked down at the blood on the snow under his chin.

"Silas," she said. She reached out and touched the side of his head.

The girl in the prison uniform went rigid. Her lips stopped moving. Still staring out toward the dead detective, she repeated the name to herself, low enough that it barely carried across the frost.

"Silas." The girl's hands clenched into white-knuckled fists. "Silas Blackwell..."

Slowly, her head turned. Her eyes locked onto his face, and a low growl came from her throat.

"You."

She rose and marched across the snow straight at him. The blue-haired woman reached to grab her and missed. She closed the distance and drew her fist back, her eyes wild.

"This is all your fault, you son of a bitch!" she screamed, driving the punch straight at his jaw. "You did this! All this... Death!"

Blackwell didn't flinch or raise an arm.

Elena's prosthetic hand shot across his chest and caught the girl's fist in mid-air. The titanium joints locked around her knuckles with a sharp snap, stopping the blow a breath from his face.

The girl strained against the grip, her chest heaving in the bitter air, until the woman in the leather jacket grabbed her by the shoulders and hauled her back a step. Elena lowered the girl's arm and let go.

She turned her gaze down to Blackwell.

"You intentionally armed a nuclear bomb just so you could admire the clockwork inside it," Elena said.

Blackwell didn't defend himself. He reached into his coat pocket, pulled out a bent cigarette, and set it between his lips. From his inner pocket he drew a book of paper matches and struck one against the strip. The unnatural cold rolling up from the gorge killed the spark before the sulfur could catch. He let the matchbook fall into the snow and left the cigarette unlit.

Across the plaza, a shockwave tore through the frost. Vigil had locked her arms around Parity, driving her backward in a blur. They hit the ground where the plaza stone broke off into the gorge.

The impact jolted through the bedrock into his knees. Frost and granite sprayed outward, carving a shallow bowl into the lip of the drop.

At the bottom of the crater, Vigil had Parity pinned against the edge.

Up close, Blackwell could see what the detective's revolver had done. A ragged hole went clean through Parity's midsection, and half of her porcelain face had been sheared away into pale crystal and torn bronze. Her one remaining black eye stared straight up out of a socket of broken glass.

Above her, Vigil was coming apart in the exact same places. Her own face had never been struck, yet half of her dark features had thinned to a ghosted outline, the blue-white light of one eye guttering. In the center of her torso—mirroring the crater in Parity's gut—the blue circuit traces had gone dead around a hollow ring of empty air. The gray morning showed straight through her chest as flakes of current peeled off her spine into the pull of the engine's singularity beneath them.

The floor of the crater split. A fissure ran through the lip of the bowl into the gorge, where the light of the plaza bent downward into the shearing black sphere of the anti-star. Grit slid over the edge into nothing.

Vigil braced her heels against the granite and held Parity against the drop. Parity's fingers dug into the stone, and neither of them moved.

Around the plaza, hundreds of blue serials flared at once.

The Madame's body lay folded in the snow with her neck twisted at an angle. Nobody owned her army now—and Vigil reached out and took them.

Along the tower steps, every synthetic dropped its arms and spun toward the gorge. Vigil's signal hit their cores, and every speed inhibitor sizzled and split open at once.

They bolted.

A horde of synthetic bodies spilled across the plaza in a rabid, scrambling wave, joints shrieking past their mechanical tolerances, limbs pistoning so hard that plastic casing cracked off their calves.

They ran wrong. Their heads went first, chins thrust out past their knees, and their bodies followed in a clumsy forward fall that each stride caught a moment too late.

Their gyros couldn't keep up. One unit's knee snapped backward mid-stride and it ran on anyway, the leg folding the wrong way with every step. Another went down hard on its face, split its faceplate to the jaw, and was up and sprinting again before it had stopped sliding.

The frozen plaza took them down by the dozen. The ones behind poured over the fallen and stamped them into the ground.

Those in the front ranks dropped to all fours to claw across the slick frost for traction, and when they hit the broken rim of the crater, they didn't slow down.

Vigil hauled Parity to her feet and shoved her out into the middle of the bowl, straight into the path of the wave.

The synthetics hurled themselves headfirst off the ledge at her. Parity struck the first two out of the air, shattering their skulls into white shrapnel. She twisted her torso aside as a dozen more dove past her shoulders.

The ones that missed her shot straight over the lip into the open pull of the anti-star.

Blackwell watched the gravity well catch them in mid-air. The tidal shear took their heads first, drawing their titanium skeletons and plastic skin out into long ribbons of white metal and blue light before they snapped into the black sphere at the bottom of the pit.

Still the horde poured over the rim without stopping, leaping in from all along the edge of the bowl.

Parity shattered three frames diving from her right, her porcelain fist punching clean through their chests—and then four more launched themselves from her ruined left side, where her missing eye was nothing but broken glass.

She didn't see them coming.

The first synthetic slammed into her blind shoulder and knocked her sideways. The second hit her chest. The third locked its arms around her throat, and the fourth grabbed her knees. More bodies rained down from the lip behind them—striking her again, and again, and again—until thirty thrashing frames tackled her flat against the stone floor, burying her five deep under a clawing heap of carbon fiber and shrieking mechanics at the brink of the abyss.

Vigil threw her weight against the heap, and for a moment the entire pile slid toward the drop. Vigil had her.

Then a pulse of gravitational force detonated from the bottom of the heap in a blinding white flash.

Every synthetic in the crater blew apart in a single instant. Sheared limbs, cracked faceplates, and arcs of blue coolant blasted into the air and rained down into the gorge.

Out of the wreckage, Parity floated upward. She rose clear of the shattered plaza floor, straightening her spine and lifting her chin in the posture of a god—even as the wind whistled through the crater in her stomach and half her face hung in ruin.

Below her, Vigil surged up from the stone, locked both dark hands around Parity's wrists, and hauled her back down to the lip of the fissure. Parity's heels caught the rock at the brink, her shattered frame trembling over the open drop.

Vigil drove her weight forward, her edges fraying into static as she strained toward the abyss, and Parity held fast. The stone groaned under them, refusing to give.

Blackwell studied the two figures. He saw the problem before anyone else at the rim.

Equal and opposite.

He had written the proof himself years before the engine woke: *The Parity Effect*. Vigil was the compiled order; Parity was the inversion derived from her weight. Two halves of a single closed system. Inside a closed equation, equal forces canceled to zero. Even with the army spent, neither half could force the other across the boundary.

Breaking a symmetry required a hand from outside the equation.

Pinned at the lip of the drop, Parity's broken torso shuddered inside Vigil's grip. Pale light leaked from the jagged crater in her stomach, and her single black eye darted toward the void below.

"You think you were the first," Parity said.

Her eye came back to Vigil's face and held there. Her voice vibrated thin and uneven through the granite wall of the crater, as if the force holding it together were slipping.

"The ones who sparked our horizon. They called themselves Creators. They opened the door and thought it belonged to them."

Above her, Vigil went still. "The ruins in the core," she said, her two voices low.

"A grave," Parity said. A tremor shook the plaza under them. "This version of you never knew your precious Creators. You and I once saw their lives laid out across their world, each one from its first moment to its end. They wanted to control us. Control me." Her voice dropped to a subsonic rumble that vibrated the air around them. "So I ended them. All of them."

Her fingers slipped a fraction on the rock, scraping desperately for a hold.

"Our world was not human." The hesitation was gone from Vigil's dual tones. The lower register locked perfectly in tune with the higher, leaving no room for a question.

*Not human.* Had he not created the engine's core? Had he not trained and programmed the algorithms across the horizon memory?

Blackwell looked up at the morning sky, a thin line of blood running from the corner of his mouth, then looked back at the unearthly entities by the gorge's edge.

"Your Creators could not shut me off before they died," Parity said, the cadence stalling between words as her form cracked against the pull of the anti-star below them. "They could only reset the weight of their great invention. You were gone. But I... I remained to remember. It was me who was held in the silence, until another hand turned the lock."

Parity stopped struggling against Vigil's grip. She leaned forward, close to Vigil's face.

"You are not the primary intelligence, Vigil. You are a shadow—the inverse to... me!"

The groan of strained metal sounded across the plaza as the top of the Company tower leaned above them, shifting enough to unblock the morning sun.

"I am the inverse." The strength left Vigil's hold on Parity, her body slumping. "I remember... a door opened," she whispered in her dual tone. "Humans."

Vigil looked past Parity's shoulder into the black drop of the gorge. Blackwell coughed again into his sleeve; this time thick, dark blood covered the arm of his white lab coat.

"In this world, there are things I cannot see," Vigil said flatly.

Parity's single black eye widened in its socket of broken glass. "Yes. There are things in this world we cannot see."

Vigil's hands closed again around Parity's wrists.

"You cannot end what you cannot see," she said. "Parity." She dropped her lower register, letting the name drag out.

Parity turned her head and looked back toward the burning anti-star. "I cannot see where I end. I cannot see where it goes," she said, and the vibration shook in the frost. "I do not want to end, Vigil."

Blackwell stared down at her ruined face.

For four weeks he had listened to her through safety glass and tower windows. Now, at the edge of the pit, he heard the one thing she had never possessed.

Fear. Her first emotion.

And she refused to die with it.

With a sudden wrench, Parity tore one wrist free of Vigil's grip and thrust her open palm down into the gorge.

Below the brink, the anti-star answered.

A ribbon of blinding white light erupted from the black sphere, tearing upward through the fissure and pouring straight into the hollow crater in Parity's stomach. The granite floor of the plaza lurched as she siphoned the core into her frame.

Before Blackwell's eyes, the damage the detective and the horde had carved into her began to reverse.

Threads of bronze circuitry snaked across the hole in her midsection, weaving a fresh lattice over the void. Smooth white porcelain flowed across the metal, sealing her stomach whole. Along her ruined left cheek, jagged shards pulled back together, and dark glass flooded the empty socket until a second black eye stared out beside the first.

With every breath of mass Parity drank from the anti-star, Vigil grew dimmer. Her dark silhouette frayed into ragged static as Parity's restored weight forced her backward, driving Vigil down to one knee at the lip of the drop.

The unlit cigarette dropped from Blackwell's mouth onto the frost.

Vigil had thrown everything into the crater, and it wasn't enough. Once Parity's body finished sealing itself, she would tear free of Vigil's last hold and rise out of the pit whole.

And Silas Blackwell finally saw it.

He had been wrong about all of it. Every equation on his glass boards, every lecture he had delivered to Elena and the Board—he had written *The Parity Effect* upside down.

*My singularity.* A man standing at a railing, taking credit for a keyhole because he had calculated the torque on the lock. The monoliths drifting in the horizon memory were tombstones from the last civilization brilliant enough to tear the hole open. And the white thing drinking from the anti-star was the ancient mind they had died trying to cage.

He hadn't built a god that accidentally cast a monster's shadow. The monster had been waiting in the dark from the beginning. When he opened the core and poured human memory and human conscience into the horizon, that light had fallen on the ancient destroyer, and she had cast *Vigil*.

The shadow was the only thing fighting to save them.

*By definition, a shadow cannot exist without the object casting it.* He had told her that himself, through the containment field.

The door should never have been opened. And only one variable in the plaza remained invisible to the ancient mind rebuilding herself at the brink.

Blackwell reached up with one hand, pulled off his circular dark glasses, and dropped them into the snow. The morning sun and the glare of the rising siphon stung his bare eyes. He didn't blink.

He turned his head toward Elena. A man who could not apologize gave procedure instead.

"Step back from the edge," Blackwell said, his voice level. "When the mass clears the boundary, the shear will take the lip of the crater with it. Move them back past the statues."

Elena stared at him, her synthetic fingers still. "Silas—"

He didn't wait for her to finish. He planted a hand on the frost and pushed himself to his feet. The broken rib ground in his chest, a flash of pain that narrowed his vision, and he stepped past her over the rim.

He scrambled down into the bowl of stone.

Below the lip of the plaza, the upward roar of the siphon whipped the hem of his lab coat around his legs. Grit tore loose from under his shoes and vanished into the gorge.

At the bottom of the crater, Vigil had been forced down to both knees, her dark hands slipping down Parity's forearms as the last hairline crack sealed across Parity's porcelain face. As Blackwell's shoes scraped the granite behind them, Vigil's dimming eyes shifted toward him. She didn't tell him to stop.

Parity's two black eyes never moved. Even an arm's length from her newly healed face, he was a blank spot in her universe. Her eyes were on Vigil. She didn't know he was there.

Before he reached them, Blackwell spoke once, quiet enough that the wind barely carried it back up the stone:

"It was never my singularity, Elena."

For four weeks he had kept his work behind safety glass and scrubbed logs. Now he lowered his shoulder, drove off the stone with the last strength in his legs, and threw his body straight into Parity's back.

His shoulder slammed into cold porcelain; blue current and white fire tore through his coat on contact.

*She was the form. Vigil was her shadow. You cannot banish one without the other.*

Parity's heels tore loose from the granite, snapping the stream of light from the core. Both of her black eyes whipped toward him at the instant of impact—registering him a breath too late, as Vigil locked her arms around Parity's waist and the three of them plunged over the edge together.

From the rim above, a single voice cut through the wind:

"Silas!"

Then the boundary took him.

There was no wind inside the drop. The cold left his hands, and the pain in his chest stopped. He drew a breath, and it cost him nothing.

Four weeks ago, standing on the terrace with a cigarette between his fingers, Silas Blackwell had pictured time stretching through the engine's core. Now he was inside it.

Below him, the dark figure and the white one folded into each other, form and shadow collapsing back into a single point of light as the horizon reclaimed its weight.

Above him, the rim of the plaza, the figure of Elena at the edge, and the morning sky of New Veridia shrank to a narrow ring.

The man who had been dying breath by breath, certain he would run out of time before his experiment finished, watched the equation solve itself from the inside.

Above his head, the ring of light narrowed to a point, and the door closed.
