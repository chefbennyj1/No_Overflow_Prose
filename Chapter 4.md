A thin, unbroken stream of white smoke spiraled upward from the cigarette pinched between Blackwell's fingers. He sat motionless in the dark of his private office at the Mnemosyne lab, watching as the smoke drifted across the surface of his desk and wound directly through the center of a high-fidelity holographic projection.

The young man in the projection wore a dark blue jacket with a high square collar. The badge on his lapel read Renewal Officer.

The smoke disrupted the hard-light matrix. The flickering light of the corrupted projection illuminated the angular lines of Blackwell's face and cast shadows against his white hair.

"My office?" Blackwell's voice was dead flat. "What exactly did they access?"

The young manager on the display shifted uncomfortably. Digital artifacts crawled at his edges. "They accessed—" The young man's eyes went somewhere off-frame. "Everything, Doctor."

Blackwell took a slow, deliberate drag from his cigarette. The end flared bright red, reflecting briefly in the black glass of his lenses. His voice remained quiet. The sudden shift in his posture was imposing. "The Renewal's incompetence has jeopardized my work."

"The Corporation is treating this breach as a critical priority," the manager replied quickly, his tone practiced but tight with underlying panic. "Sweeps are active in the Dead Circuits as we speak. Redacted logs from your terminal have been forwarded to Oshima PD to aid in the hunt."

Blackwell didn't care about the police. He didn't care about the Corporate Renewal agents kicking down doors. He cared about the fragile architecture of his creation. 

Blackwell exhaled another cloud of smoke that drifted toward the ceiling. "We shall see what is truly compromised by this idiocracy."

He tapped a key on his console. The manager’s avatar collapsed into a single, bright point of light before vanishing, plunging the room back into darkness.

Doctor Blackwell emerged from his private office and stepped directly out into the lab. 

It had been six weeks since the engine was brought online, and the toll was obvious. He surveyed the floor with quiet contempt.  Technicians slept in shifts, and the dress code had gone sometime around the fourth week. The synthetics were the only things in the room the engine hadn't changed.

Standing before a glowing interface was Elena Marlowe. The cool light of the monitors shone against her white lab coat. She was typing furiously, her face set in intense frustration, devoid of the commanding composure she normally maintained.

"The engine's been running perfectly for weeks," Elena said, not looking up from her console as Blackwell's footsteps echoed on the metal grating. "Today, we tried shutting down part of the horizon memory."

"And?" Blackwell's voice was harsh, pulling an unlit cigarette from his coat pocket and placing it between his lips.

"It ignored the command," Elena said, her voice tight. 

A few yards away stood Iris, the Horizon Boundary Analyst. She adjusted her black-rimmed glasses, the lenses reflecting the light from the screens in the lab. She swallowed hard. "The horizon boundary is working independently... some kind of advanced learning algorithm." 

Blackwell watched her analyze the data. She cleared her throat, then continued, "We don't control the wave of permutations being rendered in the core." She paused for a moment. "It's processing these changes at a godly speed."

"Do not use theology to describe the computing," Blackwell snapped, turning his circular sunglasses toward the analyst. He already knew the shutdown command wasn't an error. Iris was getting too close. Left permutivity. The anomaly he was using to train the shadow process in the core. "It is not 'godly'," he said.

Iris's eyes went wide as she stared past her monitors, watching the screens spill data down the pillars in the pit. "The algorithm is processing so fast, the synthetics on the floor can't even parse the telemetry anymore."

Blackwell smirked, stepping up to the railing and staring down into the mist at the base of the pillars. "Because you are measuring its life using our clock. Inside the core, gravity drags time into an abyss." He exhaled. "While we were out for lunch, that machine lived through civilizations worth of generations."

"I don't care how many centuries it thinks it's lived!" Elena shouted over the low, rhythmic drone of the servers. She slammed her hand against the holographic interface. "I’m forcing a hard cycle shutdown."

Blackwell didn't move to stop her. He watched the pale light from the pillar readouts filtering through the mist below. Amidst the telemetry, the shadow process flared again. 

He caught the gaze of a synthetic near the server racks. He didn't nod this time—he didn't need to. The machine's eyes had already dimmed in mid-erasure before his focus even landed. It silently linked with a second unit across the room, and for the span of a breath, neither moved.

From her workstation, Iris tilted her head, her eyes narrowing at the pillar display. Her hand hovered over her tablet. Blackwell watched her in silence. A second later, she shook her head and looked back down. The anomaly was already lost in the scroll.

Then the shadow process was gone from the logs.

The glow of Elena's monitors flashed an angry red. Across the floor, every workstation synchronized the same alert. The human technicians stepped back from their consoles, while the synthetics froze in place.

"Horizon memory shutdown reads as... unsuccessful, ma'am," Iris said, staring up at Elena. 

Blackwell turned. "The living generally don't want to die," he stated. It wasn't a philosophical observation, it was built into the nervous system of any living thing.

"Dammit!" Elena pounded a fist against the metal console, ignoring him. "Why won't the memory—?"

"Instrumental Convergence, Elena."

Elena spun around to face him, the scarlet light of the failed shutdown reflecting in her golden eyes. She hadn't misunderstood him this time. "Ha! Instrumental Convergence? Silas, you can't be serious. You can't scale a soul."

Blackwell finally turned his head to look at her. The harsh shadows carved deep lines into his face. "I'm very serious. We didn't give it tasks... we gave it a purpose."
