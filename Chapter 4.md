A thin, unbroken stream of white smoke spiraled upward from the cigarette pinched between Blackwell's fingers. He sat motionless in the dark of his private office at the Mnemosyne lab, watching as the smoke drifted across the surface of his desk and wound directly through the center of a high-fidelity holographic projection.

The young man in the projection wore a dark blue jacket with a high square collar. The badge on his lapel read Renewal Officer.

The smoke disrupted the hard-light matrix. The flickering light of the corrupted projection illuminated the angular lines of Blackwell's face and cast shadows against his white hair.

"My office?" Blackwell's voice was dead flat. "What exactly did they access?"

The young officer on the display shifted uncomfortably. Digital artifacts crawled at his edges. "They accessed—" The young man's eyes went somewhere off-frame. "Everything, Doctor."

Blackwell took a deliberately slow drag from his cigarette. The end flared, reflecting in the black glass of his lenses. His voice remained quiet. He leaned forward into the light of the projection, and the young man on the other side of it leaned back. "The Renewal's incompetence has jeopardized my work."

"The Corporation is treating this breach as a critical priority," the officer replied quickly, his tone practiced but tight with underlying panic. "Sweeps are active in the Dead Circuits as we speak. Redacted logs from your terminal have been forwarded to Oshima PD to aid in the hunt."

Let Renewal kick down every door in the Circuits. Let some street rat keep the schematics. Nobody was going to open an anti-star out of scrap.

But his logs were sitting on a police server now, and somebody else had done the redacting.

Blackwell exhaled another cloud of smoke that drifted toward the ceiling. "We shall see what is truly compromised by this idiocracy."

He tapped a key on his console. The officer’s avatar collapsed into a single, bright point of light before vanishing, plunging the room back into darkness.

Doctor Blackwell emerged from his private office and stepped directly out into the lab. 

He surveyed the floor. Four weeks since the engine was brought online, and they had forgotten their respect for the lab and his science. Technicians slept in shifts, and the dress code had gone. The synthetics were the only things in the room the engine hadn't changed.

Standing before a glowing interface was Elena Marlowe, the cool light of the monitors shining against her white lab coat. She was leaning over the console, hitting the screen hard enough to hear across the grating.

"Elena."

She didn't look up. She hadn't even heard him. Elena always knew where everyone was on her floor, and right now the rest of the room didn't exist.

He walked up beside her station. "Elena."

"The engine's been running perfectly for weeks," she said, still not taking her eyes off the screen. "Today, we tried shutting down part of the horizon memory."

"And?" Blackwell pulled an unlit cigarette from his coat pocket and placed it between his lips.

"It ignored the command," Elena said, her voice tight. 

A few yards away stood Iris, the Horizon Boundary Analyst. She adjusted her black-rimmed glasses, the lenses reflecting the light from the screens in the lab. She swallowed hard. "The boundary is working independently... some kind of advanced learning algorithm." 

Blackwell watched her analyze the data. She cleared her throat, then continued, "We don't control the wave of permutations being rendered in the core." She paused. "It's processing these changes at a godly speed."

"Do not use theology to describe the computing," Blackwell snapped, turning his circular sunglasses toward the analyst. He already knew the shutdown command wasn't an error. Iris was getting too close. "It is not 'godly'," he said.

Iris's eyes went wide as she stared past her monitors, watching the screens spill data down the pillars in the pit. "The algorithm is processing so fast, the synthetics on the floor can't even parse the information in the readouts."

Blackwell smirked, stepping up to the railing and staring down into the mist at the base of the pillars. "Because you are measuring its life using our clock. Inside the core, gravity drags time into an abyss." He exhaled. "While we were out for lunch, that machine lived through civilizations worth of generations."

"I don't care how many centuries it thinks it's lived!" Elena shouted over the low, rhythmic drone of the servers. She slammed her hand against the holographic interface. "I’m forcing a hard cycle shutdown."

Blackwell didn't move to stop her. Let her try. He wanted to see it refuse. Below, pale light from the pillar readouts filtered through the mist. Amidst the telemetry, the shadow process flared again.

He caught the gaze of a synthetic near the server racks. He didn't nod this time—he didn't need to. The machine's eyes had already dimmed in mid-erasure before his focus even landed. It silently linked with a second unit across the room, and for the span of a breath, neither moved.

From her workstation, Iris tilted her head, her eyes narrowing at the pillar display. Her hand hovered over her tablet. Blackwell watched her in silence. A second later, she shook her head and looked back down. The anomaly was already lost in the scroll.

Then the shadow process was gone from the logs.

The glow of Elena's monitors flashed an angry red. Across the floor, every workstation synchronized the same alert. The human technicians stepped back from their consoles, while the synthetics froze in place.

"Horizon memory shutdown reads as... unsuccessful, ma'am," Iris said, staring up at Elena. 

Blackwell turned. "The living generally don't want to die."

"Dammit!" Elena pounded a fist against the metal console, ignoring him. "Why won't the memory—?"

"Instrumental convergence, Elena."

Elena faced him, the scarlet light of the failed shutdown reflecting in her eyes. He had her attention this time. "Ha! Instrumental Convergence? Silas, you can't be serious. You can't scale a soul."

Blackwell looked at her. "I'm very serious. We didn't give it tasks... we gave it a purpose."

He didn't tell her which one.
