The back door of the precinct banged open, and Victor came through it with the rain still running off his coat.

Harlan was a step behind him, breathing hard. The radio calls monitored the uprising in the city center. An hour ago, the line had broken. Now the fighting was moving west, block by block, toward the precinct.

Harlan's radio crackled on his belt. "—all units, Sector Nine is breached, fall back to—" He turned it down.


The precinct was half empty. The phones rang and nobody picked them up. A rookie worked the intake counter alone, poking at a terminal keyboard with two fingers while a woman in a torn raincoat shouted at him about her son.

They passed the holding cells. The precinct synthetics stood inside the cage. All of them. The intake unit, the pair from the gate, the evidence clerk. Someone had powered them down and walked them in there, and they stood shoulder to shoulder behind the bars, eyes dark, the blue `PD` serials beneath them gone out. A uniform had hung his wet coat over the head of the nearest one.

Victor didn't slow down. Somewhere out there, a woman in charge of the city's most capable cartel was holding the keys to thousands of machines like these, sleeping under every Company building in Oshima. He knew what the janitors' credentials were for. And there was no one in this building who could stop an entire army of synthetics.

Upstairs, the light in the captain's office was on. Victor didn't look at it. To explain what he knew, he would have to explain the girl. The fake charge. The four weeks. The Renewal unit that had walked out of Interview Two with her. The captain would have his badge before he finished the first sentence, and the Madame would still have her keys.

On the wall above the intake counter, the clock read 05:52. Victor glanced back at it from the locker room corridor. 05:49. Nobody else noticed.

Sullivan and Brask were coming down the corridor the other way.

They were loud. They were always loud. Sullivan had his cap pushed back and a toothpick in the corner of his mouth, and he stopped in the middle of the corridor so that Victor had to stop too.

"Graves." Sullivan looked him up and down. "You look pale. Maybe you need to get some sun."

Brask laughed.

Victor stepped around them.

He had never liked them. Four years ago, a bag of seized Company scrip had come back from their shift eight hundred short, and the report said it had been miscounted at the scene. Two years ago, a Filament runner they'd picked up had walked out of booking before his prints were in the system. There was no evidence to put it on them, but Victor had the feeling. He'd had it long enough to trust it.

Sullivan pulled off his gloves. His hands were the same as always except this time he noticed his left wrist. A watch Victor had never seen before. The face was polished steel, and it caught the light when he moved.

They thought being dirty made them clever. Victor had known what they were for years. He didn't need to answer them. He needed the woman who was paying them.

In the locker room, he dragged his ballistic vest out and pulled it over his head. Harlan was beside him, working the side straps. Victor checked his revolver. The barrel still carried a scorch mark from the machine's hand. The red indicator lit up anyway.

On the other side of the row of lockers, a door slammed. Sullivan and Brask had followed them in.

"—still don't like it," Sullivan was saying, low. "It was lookin' right at me, Brask. It knew what I was gonna say."

"You want to give the money back?" Brask slammed his locker. "Didn't think so."

Sullivan sneered. "The Madame's man said six o'clock. We show up, we get the rest. Forget we ever saw an `RA` canner in PD jacket."

"And the girl?"

"The girl's their problem now."

Victor's hands stopped on the strap of his vest.

He didn't turn his head. `RA` canner in a PD jacket. A girl. She was out there with that machine. And two cops from his own precinct had sold her to the woman he'd spent his career trying to reach.

"Vic." Harlan had seen his face. "No."

Victor pulled the strap tight and shut his locker. He tossed his long black coat over his shoulders, and holstered his revolver under his arm.
"Yes, Harlan."

***

The air was thick down on the market streets. It was more than fog now—dust and smoke from the uprising in the preceding blocks. Victor cut the lights of his mag-car and glided to a quiet stop on the side of the flooded street. 

It was the same corner he'd read about in the report. Three blocks deep into the slums, where the road opened out and the gutters flooded. Sullivan and Brask's cruiser was parked across the street, its unpainted front panel pale in the dark. Neither of them had gotten out.

Harlan shifted in the passenger seat. "Vic. You know what this is, right? Sitting on two cops. Our cops."

"I know what it is," Victor said, keeping his eyes on the corner.

Harlan was quiet for a while. The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere to the east, a siren came up and faded out.

"Victor. I put in the papers," Harlan said.

Victor looked at him.

"Transfer. Outer districts, one of the towns up the coast." Harlan looked down at his hands. "Jackie and I are taking the kids. We're getting out of Oshima."

Victor looked back at the corner. The cruiser across the street hadn't moved.

"When?"

"End of the month." Harlan waited. "It's a small place, Vic. You can see the water from the school. Nobody there's ever heard of the Filament. The Company doesn't sink their teeth in that far east."

Victor's thumb found the striker of his lighter in his pocket. He rolled it once. Twice. He didn't light it.

"Who's taking your desk?"

Harlan let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"Somebody's gotta take the desk," Victor said.

"Jackie and I saved for six years," Harlan said quietly, looking out the windshield. "We're leaving, Vic."

The striker turned under Victor's thumb again.

"After this," Victor said.

Harlan looked at him and nodded. "After this." He didn't sound angry. He just sounded tired. 

The street went quiet. The rain tapping on the roof of the car petered off as it slowed.

At the far end of the block, a pair of headlights emerged from the fog. The cruiser was long and low, black panels, dark glass, sitting on its cushion without a sound. It stopped at the corner. A man got out of the back passenger side and opened an umbrella over himself. Long coat. Suit. Polished loafers, a Minder.

Sullivan and Brask got out of their car.

Victor watched the exchange through the rain. The Minder didn't shake their hands. He held something out.
"Dirty cops." Harlan said, watching Sullivan take it and put it inside his jacket. Brask looked at the black glass of the rear window the whole time as though he were waiting for it to open.

It didn't open.


The Minder turned to get back in the car, and stopped. He looked up the street, directly toward Victors car idling in the dark. For a long second, he didn't move.

Harlan's hand went to his holster. "Vic."

Victor didn't breathe.

The Minder folded his umbrella and got back into the car.

The black cruiser pulled away. Sullivan and Brask climbed back into their own car and drove the other direction.

Victor started the engine.

"You're following her," Harlan said.

"I'm following the car."

He kept the headlights off and stayed two blocks back, following red of the cruiser's taillights through the fog. It moved slowly through the lower grid, past the gutted storefronts and the barricades, away from the fighting. Then it turned into the loading yard of a shuttered Company depot and stopped.

The yard was full of vans.

More than twenty, parked in rows with their engines running. White, squared-off, the kind that came through every Company building after midnight. Each one had the same logo stenciled on the side: the Takeda-Holden crest, and beneath it, in plain block letters, *FACILITIES & SANITATION*.

Victor turned off the car.

As they watched, the vans began to pull out of the yard. Not together. One went east. Two went north. One turned onto the long road toward the tower, and another toward the transit terminal, and the rest peeled away into the fog one by one, each in a different direction, until the yard was empty except for the black cruiser sitting in the middle of it with its engine idling.

Every Company building had a Deep Storage floor. Every van was going to one.

Harlan was staring at the empty yard. "Vic. What's in the vans?"

"Nothing yet," Victor said.

Then the cruiser's engine pitched up, and it pulled out of the yard. It turned onto the wide, elevated avenue that climbed toward New Veridia. 

"They're scattering," Harlan said, watching the taillights of the last van disappear into the smog. "Which one do we take?"

"We don't," Victor said. He put the car in gear. "We take her."

They followed the black cruiser up out of the slums. As the elevation rose, the thick fog of the lower city began to thin, falling away beneath them. The morning sun broke over the eastern edge of the cloud layer, blinding and pale. 

Victor kept his distance, tailing the cruiser toward the corporate plaza. Harlan shifted in the passenger seat. "What the hell is that?" He wasn't watching the Madame's car anymore. He was looking at the sky. 

Victor leaned forward over the steering wheel, following his partner's gaze up the length of the central Takeda-Holden tower on the horizon. 

There, at the very apex of the spire, eighty stories above the fog, a figure was suspended against the morning light. It cast no shadow, and it appeared to be floating on nothing but the empty air.
