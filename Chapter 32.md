The back door of the precinct banged open, and Victor came through it with the rain still running off his coat.

Harlan was a step behind him, breathing hard. The radio calls monitored the uprising in the city center. An hour ago, the line had broken. Now the fighting was moving west, block by block, toward the precinct.

Harlan's radio crackled on his belt. "—all units, Sector Nine is breached, fall back to—" He turned it down.


The precinct was half empty. The phones rang and nobody picked them up. A rookie worked the intake counter alone, poking at a terminal keyboard with two fingers while a woman in a torn raincoat shouted at him about her son.

They passed the holding cells. The precinct synthetics stood inside the cage. All of them. The intake unit, the pair from the gate, the evidence clerk. Someone had powered them down and walked them in there, and they stood shoulder to shoulder behind the bars, eyes dark, the blue `PD` serials beneath them gone out. A uniform had hung his wet coat over the head of the nearest one.

Victor didn't slow down. Somewhere out there, a woman in charge of the city's most capable cartel was holding the keys to thousands of machines, even more dangerous than these, sleeping under every Company building in Oshima. He knew what the janitors' credentials were for. And there was no one in this building who could stop an entire army of synthetics.

Upstairs, the light in the captain's office was on. Victor didn't look at it. To explain what he knew, he would have to explain the girl, the fake charges, and the Renewal unit that had walked out of Interview Two with her. The captain would have his badge before he finished the first sentence, and the Madame would still have her keys.

Sullivan and Brask were coming down the corridor the other way.

They were loud. They were always loud. Sullivan had his cap pushed back and a cigarette in the corner of his mouth. He stopped in the middle of the corridor so that Victor had to stop too, and breathed smoke between them.

"Graves." Sullivan looked him up and down. "You look pale. Maybe you need to get some sun."

Brask laughed.

Victor stepped around them.

He had never liked them. Four years ago, a bag of seized Company scrip had come back from their shift eight hundred short, and the report said it had been miscounted at the scene. Two years ago, a Filament runner they'd picked up had walked out of booking before his prints were in the system. There was no evidence to put it on them, but Victor had a feeling. 

Sullivan pulled off his gloves. His hands were the same as always. On his left wrist was a watch Victor had never seen before. The face was polished steel, and it caught the light when he moved.

They thought being dirty made them clever. Victor didn't need to answer them. He needed the woman who was paying them.

In the locker room, he dragged his ballistic vest out and pulled it over his head. Harlan was beside him, working the side straps. Victor checked his revolver. The barrel still carried a scorch mark from the machine's hand. The red indicator lit up anyway.

On the other side of the row of lockers, a door slammed. Sullivan and Brask had followed them in.

"—still don't like it," Sullivan was saying, low. "It was lookin' right at me, Brask. It knew what I was gonna say."

"You want to give the money back?" Brask slammed his locker. "Didn't think so."

Sullivan sneered. "The Madame's man said six o'clock. We show up, we get the rest. Forget we ever saw an `RA` canner in PD jacket."

"And the girl?"

"The girl's their problem now."

Victor's hands stopped on the strap of his vest.

He didn't turn his head. `RA` canner in a PD jacket. A girl. She was out there with that machine. And two cops from his own precinct had sold her to the woman he'd spent his career trying to reach.

"Vic." Harlan had seen his face. "No."

Victor pulled the strap tight and shut his locker. He tossed his long black coat over his shoulders, and holstered his revolver under his arm.
"Yes, Harlan."

***

The air was thick down on the market streets. It was more than fog now—dust and smoke from the uprising in the preceding blocks. Victor cut the lights of his mag-car and glided to a quiet stop on the side of the flooded street. 

Three blocks deep into the slums, where the road opened out and the gutters flooded. Sullivan and Brask's cruiser was parked across the street, mud covered its unpainted front panel, and the worn Oshima Police crest. Neither of them had gotten out.

Harlan shifted in the passenger seat. "Vic. You know what this is, right? Sitting on two cops. Our cops."

"I know what it is," Victor said, keeping his eyes on the corner.

Harlan was quiet for a while. The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere to the east, a siren sounded with a echo and faded out.

"Victor. I put in the papers," Harlan said.

Victor looked at him.

"Transfer. One of the towns up the coast." Harlan looked down at his hands. "Jackie and I are taking the kids. We're getting out of Oshima."

Victor looked back at the corner. The cruiser across the street hadn't moved.

"When?"

"End of the month." Harlan waited. "It's a small place, Vic. You can see the water from the school. Nobody there's ever heard of the Filament. The Company doesn't sink their teeth in that far east."

Victor's thumb found the striker of his lighter in his pocket. He rolled it once. Twice. He didn't light it.

"Who's taking your desk?"

Harlan let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"Somebody's gotta take the desk," Victor said.

"Jackie and I saved for six years," Harlan said quietly, looking out the windshield. "We're leaving, Vic."

The striker turned under Victor's thumb again.

"After this," Victor said.

Harlan looked at him and nodded. "After this." He didn't sound angry. He just sounded tired. 

The street went quiet. The rain tapping on the roof of the car petered off as it slowed.

At the far end of the block, a pair of headlights emerged from the fog. The cruiser was long and low, black panels, dark glass, sitting on its cushion without a sound. It stopped at the corner. A man got out of the back passenger side and opened an umbrella over himself. Long coat. Suit. Polished loafers, a Minder.

Sullivan and Brask got out of their car.

The Minder didn't shake their hands. He held something out.
"Dirty cops." Harlan said, watching Sullivan take it and put it inside his jacket. Brask looked at the black glass of the rear window the whole time as though he were waiting for it to open.

It didn't open.


The Minder turned to get back in the car, and stopped. He looked up the street, directly toward Victor's car idling in the dark. For a long second, he didn't move.

Harlan's hand went to his holster. "Vic."

Victor didn't breathe.

The Minder folded his umbrella and got back into the car.

The black cruiser pulled away. Sullivan and Brask climbed back into their own car and drove the other direction.

Victor started the engine.

"You're following her," Harlan said.

"I'm following the car."

He kept the headlights off and stayed two blocks back, following the red of the cruiser's taillights through the fog. It moved slowly through the lower grid, past the gutted storefronts and the barricades, away from the fighting.

At the next intersection, it turned. Victor took the corner a few seconds behind it.

The street ahead was empty.

He pushed the car faster. One block. Two. Every cross street ended in the same gray wall. No taillights.

"No," Victor said. He braked at the next corner and stared down both directions. "No, no, no."

"Vic." Harlan kept his voice even. "She's gone. Let it go."

Victor's hands stayed tight on the wheel. He had chased her his whole career. He had been two blocks behind her, and he had lost her in the fog.

The Madame had the janitors' keys. There was only one Deep Storage floor in the city he had seen with his own eyes, and he had walked a girl in handcuffs out of it.

"The tower," he said, and turned the car toward it.

The fog got colder the closer they came. Frost crept in from the edges of the windshield.

Then the fog ended.

They drove out of it into clear, freezing air. A month ago, the open space around the tower had been the width of the plaza. Now it ran for blocks. A fine snow drifted down over the empty streets, and in the middle of it, the Takeda-Holden tower rose out of the lower city and disappeared into the sky.

Harlan leaned forward. "What the hell is that?"

At the top of the spire, eighty stories up, a white figure hung in the air. It held something out over the edge. A man. Even from here, the white of it stung Victor's eyes.

Harlan grabbed his arm. "Vic. Behind us."

At the edge of the clearing, two red lights glowed inside the fog.

Taillights.

Victor swung the car around and rolled back toward them. The black cruiser sat half inside the fog. Both rear doors hung open. The engine was still running.

They got out.

"What the fuck?" Harlan said.

The cruiser was empty. In the ashtray between the seats, a cigarette burned at the end of a long filter.

Victor didn't move. The Minder on the corner. That long look up the street, straight at a car with its lights off.

She had known the whole time. He had driven exactly where she wanted him.

Footsteps came out of the fog.

The Madame walked into the clearing. White silk. Wire-rimmed glasses. Snow settled on her shoulders, and she didn't brush it off. She was smiling at Victor.

Two Minders stepped out of the fog on either side of her.

Harlan's hand went to his gun.

Behind her, a light came on in the fog. A serial number, glowing faint blue at the height of a face.

Then two.

Then ten.

Then the fog behind her was full of them. Hundreds of serials, row after row, all at the same height, all of them still.
